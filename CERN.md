It looks like you're diving into the "accelerator chain" at CERN. Most people know the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) as the star of the show, but it actually relies on a relay race of smaller, older machines to get particles up to speed.

Think of it like a manual transmission car: you can't just start in 5th gear. You need to shift through the lower gears first.
1. PS (Proton Synchrotron)

The "Grandfather" of the complex, the PS began operation in 1959. Back then, it was the flagship; today, it’s a crucial middleman.

    Role: It receives protons and accelerates them to about 25 GeV.

    Versatility: Beyond feeding the next ring, it sends particles to various experiments like the "Antiproton Factory."

2. SPS (Super Proton Synchrotron)

The SPS was the powerhouse of the 1970s and 80s (where the W and Z bosons were discovered).

    Role: It takes the 25 GeV protons from the PS and cranks them up to 450 GeV.

    Size: It has a circumference of nearly 7 kilometers. Once the particles reach top speed here, they are "injected" into the final, massive ring.

3. LHC (Large Hadron Collider)

The world's largest and most powerful particle accelerator.

    Role: It takes those 450 GeV protons from the SPS and pushes them to their limit—currently 6.8 TeV (Tera-electronvolts) per beam.

    The Big Event: It circulates two beams in opposite directions and smashes them together inside four massive detectors (ATLAS, CMS, ALICE, and LHCb) to study the fundamental building blocks of the universe.

If the LHC is the "Formula 1" of particle physics, the Future Circular Collider (FCC) is the next-generation hyper-car currently on the drawing board.

As of early 2026, the project has just passed a major milestone: the FCC Feasibility Study, released in March 2025, confirmed that the project is technically and financially viable.
1. The Concept: A 91 km Giant

The FCC is planned as a massive ring buried deep underground in the Geneva basin, crossing beneath Lake Geneva and the Jura mountains.

    Circumference: ~91 km (Over 3x larger than the LHC).

    Depth: 200–400 meters below the surface.

    Location: It would reuse existing CERN infrastructure (like the SPS and LHC) as its initial "kick-starters."

2. The Two-Stage Plan

CERN isn't building just one machine; they are planning a "staged" approach within the same tunnel:

    Stage 1: FCC-ee (The Higgs Factory)

        Collisions: Electrons vs. Positrons.

        Goal: Extreme precision. It will produce millions of Higgs bosons, allowing us to measure their properties with 10x more accuracy than the LHC.

        Timeline: Construction starts in the early 2030s; operation begins in the late 2040s.

    Stage 2: FCC-hh (The Energy Frontier)

        Collisions: Protons vs. Protons.

        Goal: Brute force. It aims for a collision energy of 100 TeV (compared to the LHC’s 13.6 TeV).

        Purpose: To search for Dark Matter, explain why there is more matter than antimatter, and find new particles we can't even imagine yet.

        Timeline: Expected to start in the 2070s.

3. Current Status (2026)

We are currently in a "make or break" window for the project:

    Feasibility Confirmed: The 2025 report showed no "showstoppers." Geological drilling is currently wrapping up (slated for June 2026) to finalize the 3D map of the rock it will sit in.

    The Big Decision: In 2028, the CERN Member States will make the final decision on whether to greenlight the roughly 15 billion CHF (Swiss Francs) budget for the first phase.

    Political Debate: While supported by the EU and many physicists, critics argue the cost is too high and suggest "Plan B" options like linear colliders or muon colliders.
