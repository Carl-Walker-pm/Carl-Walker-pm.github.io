01The Released, Organized

A watercolor of a quiet registry office on pale lunar dust, where a neat row of empty chairs faces a desk and a single paper rises slowly above it, unhurried and upright.

The released citizens did not organize because they were restless. They organized because their paperwork had begun to accumulate in two places at once, and no clerk could say, with confidence, which registry should keep the second copy. A citizen released from weight is still a citizen. He requires filing. The question was referred, in the usual way, to the registrar for an opinion.

The Antigravity Division, Department of Weightlessness, had issued each of them a Certificate of Release, Form HLH-A-001, and entered each name in the Registry of the Unbound. That was sufficient for departure. It was not sufficient for correspondence, for anniversaries, or for the orderly conduct of their common affairs, which continued. The need, once stated in these terms, appeared modest and entirely unavoidable to everyone concerned.

02Citizens of Two Registries

A watercolor of two tall ledger books standing open on adjoining desks, one bound firmly to its surface and one hovering a thumb's width above, while a clerk looks between them.

Every member of the party belongs, by birth or by construction, to the first registry. The Gravity Division, Department of Lunar Construction, records the plot, issues the Lunar Building Permit, Form HLH-G-001, and maintains the Registry of Plots. Plot 001, Mare Tranquillitatis, Sector G, is the first entry and remains in good standing. Every subsequent plot is entered after it, in order, without exception or favour shown.

Release adds a second registry without cancelling the first. The citizen is therefore governed twice and held once, which is the opposite of the usual arrangement. On the Moon, where gravity is 1.62 m/s squared, this distinction is not philosophical. It is measured daily, in the behaviour of soup, tools, and correspondence. The registrar keeps both figures on the same page for ease of reference.

03What a Party Is, on Paper

A watercolor of a single sheet of paper lying on a long table, weighted at its corners by four smooth grey stones, with soft light falling across its blank and patient surface.

On paper, a party is a list of persons who have agreed to be counted together for a stated purpose. It requires a name, a form, a place to keep the list, and a person willing to keep it current. Rallies are optional. Refreshments are not recorded. The list is the institution. Everything else belongs to the history of the institution, not to its definition.

The Party of Release was therefore possible from the moment the Registry of the Unbound contained more than one name. Two names make a correspondence. Three make a quorum for certain minor decisions. The founders proceeded carefully, in the belief that an institution should be no larger, on its first day, than its paperwork. No larger institution was proposed, and none was entered in the preliminary record.

04The Inconvenience of Being Ungoverned

A watercolor of small household objects drifting at different heights in a calm white room, a cup, a key, and a folded letter each travelling slowly in a separate direction.

An ungoverned condition produces practical difficulties. Released citizens missed appointments by altitude rather than by lateness. Ballots, when attempted informally, did not descend into the box provided. Decisions taken in a room dispersed with the persons who had taken them, each at a slightly different rate of ascent, none of them reachable by telephone. The difficulties were recorded individually, in plain language, without exaggeration or complaint.

No one was harmed. Inconvenience, repeated, becomes administration. The unbound required a way to agree that would survive their leaving the room, a way to dissent that could be filed, and a way to be represented in matters affecting weightlessness by persons who understood, from experience, that furniture cannot be assumed. The request was submitted as a memorandum, not as a grievance against anyone.

05A Party Without a Headquarters

A watercolor of an empty hillside of grey lunar regolith under a black sky, marked only by a small wooden signpost with no building behind it and a path that stops politely.

The party has no headquarters, for reasons of architecture. A headquarters must stay where it is built, and the membership, as a condition of membership, does not. To fix the party to one plot would misrepresent it. To build it lightly enough to follow the membership would produce, in effect, a very slow aircraft. The proposal was briefly minuted and then, sensibly, allowed to rest undisturbed.

The party therefore keeps its address in its registry. Correspondence is directed to the Department of Weightlessness, which forwards it along the Chart of Departures. Replies take longer than letters sent to the ground. This is accepted. A party of the released cannot promise speed, only eventual arrival, correctly addressed. Correspondents are asked to allow for this, and most, being released themselves, do.

06Nobody Stood Up to Speak

A watercolor of a semicircular meeting room with all chairs empty and level, while soft silhouettes hover gently near the ceiling, each holding a folded paper and listening in complete stillness.

At the preliminary meeting, nobody stood up to speak, because standing was not available to most of those present in the customary sense. Speakers were recognized by the chair, which was also unoccupied, and their remarks were submitted in writing, in the order in which they were received by the recording clerk. The clerk read each contribution in a level voice, suitable to the occasion.

The arrangement proved satisfactory. Written remarks do not interrupt one another. They can be weighed, in the older sense of the word, at leisure. The party adopted the practice permanently: its congresses are held in writing, its debates are filed rather than concluded, and its loudest disagreements remain perfectly legible years afterwards. No vote was taken that day, and no one present regarded this as haste.

07The Membership of the Air

A watercolor of faint human figures at rest at many gentle heights above a lunar plain, like swimmers in clear water, each with a small card held carefully against the chest.

Membership is open to every citizen entered in the Registry of the Unbound, whose status reads, in full, Released, provisionally, forever. No further qualification is required. The provisional nature of the release is not held against the applicant. It is, on the contrary, the one qualification all applicants are known to share. The registry does not enquire further, and the party, following the registry, does not either.

Members receive the Party Card, Form HLH-P-001, a membership book in which attendances, congresses, and silences are recorded. Cards are numbered. The first edition is limited to one hundred, not from exclusivity, but because the printer's numbering wheel, ordered in haste, contains only two digits and a certain optimism. The remaining numbers were held in reserve, in a drawer, under a suitable weight.

08Why Not a Club

A watercolor of a cosy clubroom with deep armchairs all gently tipped and empty, a fireplace drawn in pale washes, and a round table rising very slowly toward the painted ceiling.

A club was considered, and respectfully declined. A club exists for the comfort of its members in one another's company, and comfort, for the released, is a technical problem involving tethers, ceilings, and the placement of soup. The founders wished to address the condition itself, not merely to make it more sociable. The minutes record the proposal without irony, as the party records everything without irony.

A party, unlike a club, may adopt positions. It may petition the Department of Weightlessness regarding the wording of a certificate, or the hours of the registry, or the width of a departure corridor. These are modest powers. The founders considered modesty a sound beginning for an institution representing people who rise without deciding to.

09The Politics of a Condition

A watercolor of a calm horizon dividing a heavy grey landscape below from a clear pale expanse above, with a thin tether line crossing the boundary and a small flag planted exactly upon it.

The politics of the party concern weightlessness alone: its certification, its registries, its corridors, its courtesies. How a release is granted, how it is recorded, how the released are counted, consulted, and corresponded with. The party has no position on matters that stay on the ground, where other authorities are better seated. Beyond that boundary the party is silent, officially and, in practice, almost entirely.

Within its province, the party is thorough. It debates the Tether Card and its renewal. It studies the Chart of Departures for fairness of sequence. It asks whether a provisional release, renewed forever, is meaningfully provisional. These questions are not small to those who live inside them, which is the definition of politics used here.

10The First Signature

A watercolor of a hand holding a fountain pen above a broad signature line, the pen casting a long soft shadow, while the paper beneath rests untethered on a dark polished desk.

The founding document required a first signature, and for some time no one offered, because the first signature on a lunar document carries a certain finality in the Registry of Plots, where signatures are kept under weight. The clerk waited. The pen was placed in the centre of the table, equidistant from all present. An interval of some minutes passed in an orderly and unembarrassed silence.

In the end, the signature was made by the citizen whose certificate bore the lowest number in the Registry of the Unbound, on the principle that precedence should follow the record, not ambition. The handwriting is ordinary. The registrar has noted, in the margin, only the date and the observation that the pen returned slowly.

11The Founding, on Paper

A watercolor of a stack of fresh documents being squared into a neat pile by gloved hands, beside a stamp, an ink pad, and a window looking out over a still grey sea of dust.

The Party of Release was founded on paper, which is where the Hi-Tech Limited Holding conducts all of its most consequential business. There was no ceremony beyond the ceremony of completion: the form filled, the copy made, the copy of the copy filed against loss, and the original placed where originals are placed. The stamp used on that occasion is preserved with the founding papers.

Founding in this manner has advantages. A paper founding cannot be rained upon. It does not require a crowd, a platform, or a favourable wind, all of which are unreliable in the Department of Weightlessness. What it requires is accuracy. The founders supplied accuracy, checked it twice, and initialled the correction of a single comma.

12MMXXVI

A watercolor of a stone foundation tablet set into lunar dust, its carved roman numerals softened by pale light, with long morning shadows stretching across an otherwise empty and level plain.

The Holding itself was founded on paper in MMXXVI, and the party, arriving later in the same long year of the registries, adopted the date as its own point of reference. MMXXVI appears on the Party Card, on the charter, and on the minutes, in numerals that require a moment longer to read. The date is spoken aloud, when required, slowly and in full, without abbreviation.

The registrar prefers it so. A year written in roman numerals cannot be hurried. It must be counted out, letter by letter, like a staircase. For an institution whose members ascend whether they intend to or not, a staircase in the date is a useful reminder that rising, done properly, is done one step at a time.

13The First Congress, Held in Writing

A watercolor of a long table covered with envelopes arriving from many directions across a pale sky, each envelope open, each letter laid out in an orderly and widening spiral.

The first congress of The Party of Release was held in writing, in accordance with the practice adopted at the preliminary meeting. Delegates submitted their contributions by post to the Department of Weightlessness. The congress was declared open when the first envelope was opened, and remained open until the last reply was received. Attendance, for this purpose, was counted from the letters themselves, duly signed.

It lasted, by the registry clock, eleven weeks. No delegate complained. Travel to a written congress is instantaneous and its adjournment is mercifully gradual. Decisions emerged in the order the letters arrived, which the founders accepted as a form of fairness older than voting: first written, first considered, nothing lost in transit except haste. The original envelopes have been retained, as congress records, in the grey volume.

14The Minutes of the First Congress

A watercolor of a bound minute book lying open under a reading lamp, its pages filled with even lines of handwriting, beside a ruler, a blotter, and a glass of water that remains perfectly still.

The minutes of the first congress occupy one volume, bound in grey. They record attendance by correspondence, apologies from two citizens whose departures had been rescheduled by the Chart of Departures, and the full text of every contribution, including those the congress respectfully declined to adopt, which are given equal space. The volume is available for inspection by any member presenting a Party Card.

Certain entries deserve note. Item four records a silence held for the anthem, the party's anthem being a held silence, observed by all delegates simultaneously in their separate locations. The clerk has written, beneath the entry, that it was the best attended item of the congress, and the only one minuted without amendment. The observation was allowed to stand, as an accurate report of the proceedings.

15An Agenda of One Item

A watercolor of a vast empty agenda sheet pinned to a pale wall, bearing a single small mark at its centre, with rows of unused lines extending calmly above and below it.

The agenda of the first congress contained one item. This was not poverty of ambition. The founders held that a new party should decide one thing completely rather than many things approximately, and that the one thing should be the thing without which the rest could not be filed correctly in sequence. The sheet was retained and filed, as the first exhibit of the congress.

The item read, in full: that the released citizens constitute a party, under a name to be decided, for the conduct of their common affairs in weightlessness. Everything else, the charter, the card, the flag, follows from that sentence as furniture follows from a room. The congress considered it for eleven weeks, and adopted it.

16The Question of a Name

A watercolor of many small paper slips scattered gently across a dark table, each blank, drifting apart in slow symmetry around a single slip that has come to rest face down at the centre.

A name was required for the form, and the form, as the registrar observed, cannot be completed with a gesture toward the ceiling. Suggestions were submitted in writing throughout the congress. Some proposed the condition, some the place, some the direction of travel. Each was read aloud to an empty room, and filed. The room, though empty, is the customary place for such formal readings.

The founders set three tests. The name must be accurate at the registry counter. It must be dignified when read in correspondence. And it must not promise what the party cannot deliver, such as arrival, altitude, or weather. Several handsome names failed the third test, and are preserved, with respect, in an appendix. The appendix is bound separately and may be consulted on written application.

17The Name, Decided

A watercolor of a brass nameplate being lowered gently into place above a quiet doorway, while soft light from the open door falls across lunar dust that no foot has yet disturbed.

The congress adopted the name The Party of Release, without abbreviation in formal correspondence. Release is the word on the certificate, the word in the registry, and the word the citizens use for their own condition at the breakfast table. A party should be named for the fact that made it necessary. The decision was entered in the minutes in the party's new name, for the first time.

With the name came the motto, adopted in the same sitting: It rises, politely. The flag followed as a matter of course, the Chart of Departures carried as a banner, since the chart already shows where every member is going. No further emblem was thought necessary. An institution, like a traveller, should not carry more than it can hold.

18The Founding Members, Counted

A watercolor of a clerk counting a small procession of figures along a dotted line on a great sheet, each figure distinct in coat and posture, the line ending well before the edge of the page.

The founding members were counted twice, by two clerks, working from the Registry of the Unbound and from the returned congress correspondence. Where the counts disagreed, the correspondence was believed, on the principle that a citizen who has written to a congress has, for institutional purposes, unmistakably been present at it. Discrepancies of this kind are expected wherever departure affects ordinary arithmetic.

The final number is recorded in the minutes and on the first page of the charter, and is not large. The registrar notes this without concern. The Gravity Division began with a single plot, Plot 001, and has not suffered for it. Institutions are not weighed, the registrar observes, by their membership, but by their filing.

19The Charter, First Page

A watercolor of the first page of a great charter resting under glass, its opening lines rendered as soft bands of ink, watched over by a small lamp and a pair of white registrar's gloves.

The first page of the charter states what the party is, whom it represents, and under which forms it acts. It names the Hi-Tech Limited Holding, the Antigravity Division, and the Registry of the Unbound. It defines a member by the Party Card, Form HLH-P-001, and a congress by its correspondence, wherever received. Each definition is followed, in the registrar's hand, by the form number it refers to.

It also records, early and plainly, the limits of the enterprise. The party governs the affairs of the released in their capacity as released. It does not issue certificates, amend releases, or countermand the Department of Weightlessness. A charter that begins with its limits, the founders reasoned, is unlikely to be surprised by them later.

20The Charter, Last Page

A watercolor of the last page of a charter lying in evening light, mostly empty, with a wide calm margin below the final lines and a window beyond it open to a pale and rising sky.

The last page of the charter is largely empty, by design. After the signatures, the registrar has left room: room for amendments not yet imagined, for members not yet released, for congresses whose letters have not yet been written. An institution for the unbound should not close its own document too completely. The blank space is numbered, like every other page, so that it cannot be mistaken for an omission.

The final clause provides that the charter may be revised by a congress held in writing, and that the silence of the anthem shall be observed before any revision is adopted. Then the page ends. What follows, in the bound volume, is only the index, and after the index, the quiet satisfaction of a thing properly begun.

21The Seal of the Party

A round wax seal in pale blue-grey rests beside an unbound certificate on a quiet desk, a thin tether curving away toward an open, starlit window.

The Seal of the Party is kept by the Antigravity Division, Department of Weightlessness, in a shallow drawer lined with felt. It is round, unhurried, and heavier than it appears, which the Registrar considers appropriate. When pressed to a Party Card, Form HLH-P-001, it does not command the paper to stay. It records that the paper was present, and that the holder was Released, provisionally, forever.

No seal is applied in haste, and none is applied twice to the same page. If an impression is faint, the faintness is noted in the margin and allowed to stand, for the Registry of the Unbound has learned that clarity and permanence are not the same virtue. A clear seal fades evenly. A faint one is remembered longer by those who witnessed its pressing.

22The Ink of the Founding

A small glass bottle of dark ink stands on blotting paper beside a resting pen, with loose sheets drifting very slightly above the table in still air.

The Ink of the Founding was mixed once, in MMXXVI, when Hi-Tech Limited Holding was founded on paper and the Party of Release required a colour that would not insist. The recipe is filed and not discussed. The ink is dark enough to be read at a distance and light enough that no signature appears to be shouting across the page toward its reader.

A single bottle serves the founding documents, the first Party Cards, and the early entries in the Registry of the Unbound. When the level falls, no alarm is recorded. The Registrar simply writes more slowly, in smaller and more deliberate letters, so that what remains may last. In this way the founding continues, quietly, each time the pen is lifted and set down again.

23Gravity Is a Condition, Not a Policy

A lone figure stands on pale lunar ground beneath a vast still sky, a dropped stone suspended mid-fall beside an upright, untouched signpost.

Point one of the Program: gravity is a condition, not a policy. The Gravity Division measures it plainly at 1.62 m/s² and issues the Lunar Building Permit, Form HLH-G-001, accordingly. The Party of Release does not dispute the measurement. A condition may be described, recorded, and worked around, but it cannot be repealed by vote, nor improved by argument, nor blamed for remaining exactly what it is each morning.

To treat a condition as a policy would be to expect an apology from it, and none has ever been received. The Program therefore begins with restraint. Foundations in the Registry of Plots are laid with gravity in mind, and departures are prepared in the same spirit. The stone falls because falling is its condition. The citizen rises for entirely separate, properly documented reasons.

24One Tether, by Right

A single fine cord stretches from a quiet lunar plain into empty sky, its near end coiled neatly beside a small, unattended desk.

Point two of the Program: every citizen is entitled to one tether. The entitlement is not earned, renewed by performance, or withdrawn for quiet behaviour. It is entered beside the Certificate of Release, Form HLH-A-001, and acknowledged on the Tether Card. One tether is considered sufficient. More than one would suggest a citizen expected to depart in several directions at the same time.

The tether itself is unremarkable, which is its chief recommendation. It does not pull the citizen back and it does not urge the citizen onward. It simply maintains a relation between a person and a place, at whatever length the day requires. If it is never used, it is nevertheless kept ready, coiled and inspected, because a right unused is still a right held in good order.

25The Right to Let Go of It

An open hand releases a thin cord into still, star-filled air above a calm lunar horizon, the cord drifting gently without falling.

Point three of the Program: every citizen is entitled to one tether, and to letting go of it. The second half of the entitlement completes the first. A tether that cannot be released would be a different instrument entirely, and would require a different form, a different registry, and, the Registrar suspects, a considerably longer and less pleasant set of instructions for all concerned.

Letting go is recorded without ceremony and without inquiry into motive. The Tether Card is marked, the Chart of Departures is updated, and the citizen's status remains unchanged: Released, provisionally, forever. No interval must pass before the tether may be taken up again, if it is found, or replaced, if it is not. The Program regards both departure and return as ordinary administrative weather.

26The Right to Drift

A small solitary figure drifts slowly above a silent grey plain, arms relaxed, with distant low hills receding under a pale and empty sky.

Point four of the Program: the citizen has the right to drift. Drifting is distinguished from travelling, which implies a destination, and from falling, which implies an interruption. To drift is to move at the speed the situation provides, in the direction the situation suggests, for as long as the situation remains agreeable. No itinerary is filed, because none could be honestly promised in advance.

The Registry of the Unbound does not track a drifting citizen continuously, as continuous tracking would defeat the purpose of the exercise. Instead, position is noted at courteous intervals, when the citizen reports, or is reported by a friend, or passes a known landmark at a stately pace. Between such notes, the record shows a dotted line. The dotted line is considered a complete and respectable account.

27Greeted While Drifting

Two distant figures drift past one another in open stillness, one raising a hand in greeting, with a faint tether trailing far behind each.

Point five of the Program: the citizen has the right to be greeted while drifting. A greeting offered to a person in motion acknowledges that motion need not excuse anyone from courtesy. The standard form is brief, spoken clearly, and timed so as not to require the drifting citizen to stop, turn sharply, or pretend not to have heard when hearing was perfectly possible.

If two citizens drift past one another in opposite directions, each is entitled to the greeting and each is expected to return it, resources permitting. A missed greeting is not an offence and is not pursued. It is simply noted, where noting is possible, as weather is noted: it passed, it was not exchanged, and the sky, on the whole, remained entirely undisturbed by the omission.

28All Decisions in Writing

A long table holds neat stacks of letters and sealed envelopes receding into soft light, with empty chairs spaced widely and patiently apart.

Point six of the Program: all decisions are taken in writing, at a distance. Congresses are held in writing, and the Program extends the courtesy to everything smaller than a congress. A decision committed to paper can be read twice, considered once, and answered when the reader's thoughts have settled. Distance, in this arrangement, is not a defect to be corrected but the working condition itself.

The Party Card, Form HLH-P-001, is itself a written decision that its holder belongs. Nothing in the Party of Release is settled by raised voices in a shared room, because there is no shared room, and the voices, if raised, would arrive too late to be useful. Letters arrive instead. They are opened in order, answered in order, and filed without triumph.

29Nothing Decided While Falling

A sheet of paper floats level and unhurried beside a slowly tumbling chair, both suspended in calm air above a distant lunar surface.

Point seven of the Program: no decision is taken while falling. Falling, however brief, occupies the whole attention of the person to whom it is happening, and attention divided in that moment produces documents that later require apology. The Party therefore waits. The ground, or the absence of ground, will clarify the matter soon enough without assistance from hasty resolutions passed mid-descent.

A proposal that arrives during a fall is held, unread if necessary, until the citizen is either landed, recovered, or comfortably drifting. Only then is it opened, dated, and considered in the usual written manner. Citizens have observed that most urgent questions lose their urgency during a fall and regain only their proper size afterwards, like objects receding peacefully into a well-kept distance.

30Distance Is Not Disagreement

Two small houses stand far apart on a pale plain under the same wide sky, a faint dotted path of footprints curving gently between them.

Point eight of the Program: distance is not disagreement. Citizens of the Party of Release may be separated by corridors, craters, or considerable stretches of unmarked sky, and remain in complete accord on every matter the Registry records. Proximity has never been listed as evidence of agreement, and the Program declines to begin listing absence as evidence of its opposite.

Correspondence should therefore be read for what it says, not for how far it has travelled. A letter that arrives after a long interval has not been reconsidered by the distance; it has only been carried by it. When a reply is slow, the Registrar assumes the route was long. When a reply is brief, the Registrar assumes the thought was short. Both assumptions preserve the peace.

31Opposing Nothing, and No One

An empty debating hall in soft watercolour light, rows of unoccupied chairs facing one another calmly, with a single chart hanging quietly at the far end.

Point nine of the Program: the party opposes nothing and no one. Opposition would require the Party to hold still against something, and holding still against something is a posture the released citizen has, by definition, been released from. The Party maintains positions, records them in writing, and allows other positions to exist at their own documented distance without interference or commentary.

This is not indifference, and the Registrar is careful with the distinction. The Party cares for its members, its tethers, its silence, and its charts with steady attention. It simply refuses the additional labour of being against. Energy saved in this manner is redirected to correspondence, to greeting those who drift past, and to keeping the Registry of the Unbound in its usual impeccable and unhurried order.

32It Rises, Politely

A small gathering of figures lifts slowly and evenly from a quiet plain, hats still in place, beneath a broad and undisturbed morning sky.

Point ten of the Program: it simply rises, politely. The motto of the Party of Release is not a summary of this point but its entire argument, repeated for convenience on suitable occasions. Rising, when done politely, involves no announcement, no displacement of others, and no suggestion that those remaining below have made an error in judgement by staying where they are.

Politeness in ascent consists mainly of pace and clearance. One rises slowly enough to be greeted, and far enough aside to avoid the tethers of neighbours. The Chart of Departures records such ascents without adjectives. Afterwards, the plain is exactly as it was, minus one citizen, plus one entry, and the Department of Weightlessness considers the transaction closed, complete, and satisfactory to all parties concerned.

33Paper Outlasts Weather

A stack of documents rests under a smooth stone on an open plain while soft rain and wind pass around it, the pages calm and undisturbed.

Point eleven of the Program: paper outlasts weather. This has been tested involuntarily on several occasions, when storms crossed the Registry's courtyards and the citizens took shelter while the documents, properly weighted, did not. Afterwards the citizens returned, somewhat damp, and found their releases, permits, and Party Cards waiting with a composure that the Registrar can only describe as professional.

The Program draws a modest conclusion from this. Decisions written down survive the conditions under which they were made, including enthusiasm, fatigue, and sudden changes in pressure. A spoken resolution belongs to its weather and disperses with it. A filed one remains available for quiet re-reading in calmer air, which is why the Party of Release continues to conduct its entire existence on paper.

34Numbered, Not Ranked

A neat row of numbered membership books lies open on a long table, each at the same height, under even and impartial light.

Point twelve of the Program: every release is numbered; none is ranked. The Party Card, Form HLH-P-001, is numbered, and the first edition comprises one hundred, but the number records only the order in which the Registry received the citizen. Number seven is not senior to number eight. Number eight has not overtaken number seven. They are adjacent entries, and adjacency is not a competition.

The same discipline governs the Registry of the Unbound as a whole. Certificates of Release are filed in sequence and retrieved without preference, whether the citizen departed dramatically at dawn or drifted off during routine correspondence. To rank releases would require a standard of rising, and the Party has examined the idea in writing, at a distance, and respectfully allowed it to lapse.

35A Friend Holds the Tether

One figure stands calmly on pale ground holding a loose cord that rises gently into the sky, where a second small figure floats in quiet stillness.

Point thirteen of the Program: the tether is held by a friend, not by an office. An office can hold a document, a key, or a position, and hold each of them correctly. A tether, however, responds to the holder's attention in ways that procedure cannot fully describe. The friend notices slack, tension, and silence along the line, and interprets each without needing to file a report.

The arrangement is voluntary on both ends, which the Program regards as its foundation. A friend may pass the tether to another friend, provided the transfer is acknowledged and the citizen above is informed in writing when practicable. The Department of Weightlessness records who holds, but does not appoint holders. Some responsibilities, the Registrar notes, improve considerably when they are chosen rather than assigned.

36Silence, Held Together

A circle of small figures floats at ease in open air, hands resting, facing inward across a calm and unbroken expanse of quiet sky.

Point fourteen of the Program: silence, held together, is a form of speech. The anthem of the Party of Release is a held silence, performed whenever members correspond in spirit while saying nothing in particular. A silence held alone may be merely private. A silence held together has structure, duration, and agreement, and can be entered and left by mutual understanding without a word being misplaced.

During such silences, no business is transacted, because transacting business would interrupt the very item under consideration. Participants afterwards report that much was settled, though the Registry, properly, records only the date and the duration. Citizens who find the practice difficult are encouraged to begin with short silences, held in good company, and to let the quiet lengthen at its own unhurried pace.

37A Chart Is a Promise

A large paper chart spread on a table shows faint dotted routes curving into blank pale space, held flat by two resting hands at its corners.

Point fifteen of the Program: a chart is a promise the sky has not yet broken. The Chart of Departures records where citizens have gone and suggests, with appropriate humility, where citizens may go next. Each dotted route is therefore both an account and an expectation. The sky, so far, has honoured the arrangement by remaining traversable along most of the lines drawn.

When a route is not followed, the chart is not corrected in anger. The line remains, the citizen's actual course is entered beside it, and the difference is allowed to stand as information rather than failure. A promise, in the Registry's usage, is not a guarantee extracted from the future. It is a clear statement of intent, filed where the future can find it.

38Further Out, Not Higher Up

Several small figures rest at different distances across a wide horizontal sky, all at the same gentle level, with the pale ground far below them.

Point sixteen of the Program: no one is promoted upward; some are simply further out. The Party of Release maintains no ladder, because a ladder would oblige citizens to stand on one another's progress. Distance outward is measured, logged, and respected, but it confers no authority over those nearer in. The citizen furthest out receives the same letters, in the same order, somewhat later.

New members sometimes ask when advancement might be expected, and the Registrar answers with the chart. Advancement is not withheld; it is simply not the direction in use. A citizen may drift further this year than last, and the Registry will note the fact with satisfaction, exactly as it notes a citizen who has chosen, wisely and entirely permissibly, to remain precisely where they were.

39The Congress Never Convenes

A vast empty hall prepared with a long table, paper, and pens at every seat, while outside the tall windows small figures drift peacefully past.

Point seventeen of the Program: the congress never convenes; it corresponds. A congress of the Party of Release held in one room would require every citizen to descend, arrive, and sit, which would contradict several earlier points of this Program simultaneously. Correspondence avoids the difficulty entirely. Motions travel, replies travel, and the congress is considered permanently in session wherever its letters happen to be.

Minutes are therefore unusually complete, since nothing was ever said that was not also written. Disagreements, where they occur, arrive dated and signed, and can be answered after reflection rather than during applause. The Registrar has attended every congress by this method and reports the attendance excellent, the acoustics perfect, and the closing silence, held together across the distance, entirely satisfactory.

40Membership Confers Membership

A single membership book lies open on a quiet desk beside a resting tether and a folded chart, in soft and even afternoon light.

Point eighteen of the Program: membership confers nothing but membership. The Party Card, Form HLH-P-001, does not entitle its holder to precedence, exemption, or a better position in any queue the Holding maintains. It records that the holder belongs to the Party of Release, that the belonging was entered in writing, and that no additional meaning should be attached to the entry by anyone.

Citizens occasionally inquire what membership is for, if it confers nothing further, and the Registrar considers the question a healthy sign. Belonging, held plainly, is sufficient work for one document. The member is greeted while drifting, held by a friend, numbered without ranking, and counted among those who rise, politely. The Program asks for nothing more, and promises, in return, exactly this.

41The Party Does Not Grieve Trajectories

A watercolor of a released paper form drifting slowly upward over a quiet registry office, its shadow shrinking on the desk below while clerks continue their work undisturbed.

Point nineteen of the Program: what is dropped departs, and the Party does not grieve trajectories. A trajectory, once filed, is complete. It requires no ceremony of regret, no committee of looking downward, and no minute of silence beyond the anthem, which is silence in any case. The object has gone where objects go when released. The record remains where records remain.

This is not indifference. The Department records the departure accurately, enters the time, and closes the line without flourish. To grieve a trajectory would be to follow it, and to follow it would be to remain attached. The Party of Release permits fondness for what was held. It does not permit holding on by means of sorrow, however neatly documented.

42The Downward Are Colleagues

A watercolor of two groups of small figures, one standing firmly on a lunar plain and one floating gently above it, exchanging documents across the small vertical distance between them.

Point twenty of the Program: the downward are colleagues, not opponents. Citizens who remain under gravity, who file for Lunar Building Permits under Form HLH-G-001 and consult the Registry of Plots, are not the adversaries of the Unbound. They are colleagues working in another division, under another weight, with paperwork of equal seriousness and equal standing in the Holding.

The Party therefore maintains no argument with the ground. Meetings between the divisions are conducted at table height, which both sides accept as neutral territory. Correspondence is courteous, copies are exchanged, and no citizen is asked to rise or to descend for the sake of a signature. Difference of altitude is an administrative fact, not a position.

43One Foot in the Paperwork

A watercolor of a floating citizen in a quiet office, one slippered foot resting lightly on a tall stack of bound registries while the rest of the figure hovers at ease.

Point twenty-one of the Program: every citizen keeps one foot in the paperwork. Release is not an exemption from record. The Unbound citizen who has received a Certificate of Release under Form HLH-A-001 remains entered in the Registry of the Unbound, and that entry is the foot in question. It does not drag. It anchors the file, not the citizen.

In practice this means one obligation, calmly kept: keep the record current. An address may drift, a Tether Card may be renewed, a departure may be charted, but the entry must be findable. The Party asks nothing heavier than this. A citizen with one foot in the paperwork may rise with the other, and with everything else, without disorder.

44Feet Off the Ground, Records On It

A watercolor of an archive room with shelves of ledgers resting solidly on the floor while, above them, members float in an orderly row reading their own entries.

Point twenty-two of the Program: the Party keeps its feet off the ground and its records on it. The distinction is practical. Feet, in the released condition, are unreliable for storage. Records, by contrast, require a shelf, a drawer, or at minimum a flat surface that stays where it was left. The Party therefore stores nothing on its members.

Every document of the Party rests at ground level, in the custody of the Party Desk, weighted if necessary by ordinary stones cleared for the purpose. Members may consult their records during office hours, descending or sending by post as they prefer. The arrangement has never failed. What rises is the citizen. What remains is the evidence, filed flat and findable.

45The Program, Complete When Written

A watercolor of a clerk finishing the final line of a large bound program on a desk, pen lifted, while the completed pages settle under a plain glass weight.

Point twenty-three of the Program: the Program is complete when it is completely written down. There is no unwritten portion, no oral supplement, and no understanding held in reserve by senior members. If a principle of the Party of Release is not on the page, it is not a principle of the Party. It may be a habit. Habits are not binding.

With this point the Program closes, at twenty-three points, and the closing is itself recorded. Amendments, if any are ever proposed, will be written in full, numbered in sequence, and read in silence before filing. Until then the text stands as issued. Citizens are invited to rise under it exactly as written, neither adding altitude nor subtracting commas.

46The Party Card

A watercolor of a small membership book resting open on a registry desk beside a Certificate of Release and a Tether Card, all three arranged in a neat, unhurried row.

The Party Card is the membership book of the Party of Release, issued under Form HLH-P-001 by the Antigravity Division, Department of Weightlessness. It is given to a citizen whose release is complete and whose entry stands in the Registry of the Unbound. Possession of the Card records membership. It confers no additional altitude, which the citizen already has.

The Card is small enough for a pocket and large enough for its fields, which are described in the following sections. It is printed on stock chosen for durability in low gravity and lower filing cabinets. Each Card is numbered, entered, and sealed before issue. A Card that has not been entered does not exist, however convincingly it may float.

47A Book, Not a Badge

A watercolor of a citizen reading a small bound book while floating in a bright corridor, the book held open with both hands and turned at a page.

The Party Card is a book, not a badge. It is not pinned, displayed, or held up at doorways. It opens, it has pages, and its contents accumulate over the years of a membership: entries, renewals, congresses attended in writing, and departures duly charted. A badge declares. A book records. The Party prefers the second, quieter instrument.

Members are not required to show the Card on demand, because there is no demand. Another member wishing to confirm a standing writes to the Party Desk and receives an answer by post. The Card stays in its pocket, where books belong when not being read. Its authority is in its pages, not in its presentation.

48The Fields of the Card

A watercolor of an open membership book seen from above, its blank ruled pages and empty boxes rendered in soft washes, with a pencil resting across the gutter.

The fields of the Card are few and are completed in order. They are: the name of the citizen as entered in the Registry of the Unbound; the number of the Certificate of Release; the Card number; the date of issue; and the page for renewals. A final field, headed in the form simply as Remarks, receives whatever the record requires and nothing more.

No field asks for weight, for obvious reasons, and none asks for destination. Where a citizen is going is a matter for the Chart of Departures, not for membership. Fields left inapplicable are ruled through with a single line, so that an empty box is never mistaken for an oversight. The Card, when complete, reads plainly and says little.

49Card No. 001

A watercolor of a single small book displayed alone in a glass-fronted cabinet in an otherwise empty office, morning light falling across its cover.

Card No. 001 is the first Party Card, the first entry under Form HLH-P-001 and the beginning of the numbering that all later Cards follow. It was entered before any other, in the first hand the Party employed for the purpose, and it set the standard for spacing, ink, and restraint that the Department has maintained without revision since.

The Card is retained by the Party Desk and is not in circulation. It may be viewed during office hours by any member who asks in writing, and it is brought out without ceremony, placed on the desk, and returned to its drawer afterward. Its distinction is purely numerical. The Party regards this as distinction enough.

50The Seal on the Card

A watercolor of a heavy round seal press standing on a desk beside a small open book, a clerk's hand resting on the lever, ready but not yet pressing.

Each Party Card bears the seal of the Party of Release, applied once, at issue, on the page of the citizen's entry. The seal is round, unadorned, and pressed by hand at the Party Desk. It does not certify enthusiasm. It certifies that the entry above it was made by the office authorized to make it, on the date given.

A Card without the seal is a draft and is treated as correspondence until sealed. The seal is never applied twice to the same entry, never applied in advance, and never lent. If a pressing is faint, the fact is noted in Remarks and the faintness stands. The Party's records are permitted to show the pressure of the hand that made them.

51Entered by Hand

A watercolor of a clerk writing carefully in a small book with a fountain pen, an ink bottle and a blotter nearby, the rest of the office still and orderly.

All entries in the Party Card are made by hand. The Department of Weightlessness considered printed entries and set the proposal aside, on the ground that membership is personal and handwriting is the ordinary evidence of a person. The hand employed is the Party hand: upright, unhurried, and legible at arm's length, whether the arm is floating or not.

Errors are corrected by a single ruled line and a fresh entry beside it, initialled by the clerk. Nothing is erased, because nothing in a record is improved by pretending it was never written. Over the years a well-kept Card shows its corrections openly, and the Party considers this a feature of the book, not a blemish on the member.

52What the Card Proves

A watercolor of a clerk comparing an open membership book with a large registry volume on a desk, nodding once, the two books aligned side by side.

The Party Card proves three things, and the Department lists them without enlargement. It proves that the citizen named in it was released, under a Certificate of Release duly issued. It proves that the citizen is a member of the Party of Release. And it proves that the entry in the Registry of the Unbound and the entry in the Card agree.

This is sufficient for all Party purposes: for voting in a congress held in writing, for renewal, for the charting of departures, and for being remembered accurately by the institution. The Card is accepted by every office of the Antigravity Division without further question. Offices of the Gravity Division accept it as well, colleagues being colleagues, and file their acceptance.

53What the Card Does Not Prove

A watercolor of a small book resting closed on a windowsill, beside it a set of ordinary objects it makes no claim about, all lit by the same calm daylight.

The Card does not prove altitude, direction, speed, or intent. It does not prove that its holder is rising at present, has risen recently, or intends to rise again. It is not a Lunar Building Permit, confers no plot, and opens no door in the Gravity Division. It is not evidence of character. The Party keeps no such evidence and issues none.

Members occasionally ask whether the Card proves belonging. The Department answers carefully: it proves membership, which is the administrative form of belonging and the only form the Holding is equipped to certify. The rest is conducted between citizens, off the record, at whatever height they choose. The Card remains in the pocket during such occasions, proving nothing, correctly.

54Lost Cards, Drifting Members

A watercolor of a small book floating alone in a vast pale sky above a quiet landscape, while far below a clerk opens a drawer of replacement forms.

Cards are lost, usually upward. A member who has lost a Card reports the loss to the Party Desk by post, giving the Card number if remembered and the date of release, which is never forgotten. The lost number is recorded in the Registry of the Unbound as outstanding, and the member's standing continues uninterrupted. Membership is in the Registry, not in the pocket.

A replacement Card is then prepared, bearing the original number and a note in Remarks recording the loss and the date of reissue. Only one replacement is outstanding at a time. If the original Card is later recovered, drifting or otherwise, it is returned to the Desk and filed. Members are asked not to grieve the interval. See point nineteen.

55The Card Renewed by Post

A watercolor of a letterbox on a quiet street at dawn, a small parcel and several envelopes resting inside, with a clerk approaching carrying a stamp and a ledger.

The Party Card is renewed by post. When a renewal falls due, the member sends the Card to the Party Desk in the envelope provided for the purpose, which is addressed, unadorned, and large enough. The Desk enters the renewal on the renewal page, applies no new seal, and returns the Card by the next post. The interval is expected and causes no lapse in standing.

Members in active drift are advised to post early, allowing for the ordinary delays of distance. A Card sent from a considerable height may take some time to arrive, descending as it does by conventional means. The Department waits without comment. Nothing in the Party's administration has ever been urgent, and renewal least of all.

56One Card per Citizen

A watercolor of a single row of identical small books standing upright in a drawer, each spine distinct, with one empty space where a book has been taken out.

There is one Card per citizen. The rule is absolute and simplifies everything it touches. A citizen may not hold a second Card under a second entry, may not lend the Card, and may not be entered twice in the Registry of the Unbound to improve the view. One release, one membership, one book: the arithmetic of the Party is deliberately unambitious.

The rule also protects the citizen. No office can ask which of several Cards is the true one, because the question cannot arise. If a duplicate is ever discovered, the later entry is cancelled by ruled line, the later Card is filed unsealed, and the matter is closed without blame. Duplication is a clerical event, not a character event.

57The First Hundred Cards

A watercolor of ten rows of ten small books arranged on a long table in a bright hall, a clerk counting them unhurriedly with the tip of a pencil.

The first edition of the Party Card is one hundred cards, numbered in order from Card No. 001, and issued in that order as citizens completed their release and were entered. No number in the first hundred was reserved, exchanged, or held back for a citizen considered more elevated. The sequence is the honour, and the sequence is strictly first come, first recorded.

When the hundredth Card was entered, the edition closed and the fact was noted in the Registry without ceremony. Later Cards continue the numbering beyond the edition and are identical in form, stock, and standing. Members of the first hundred are listed together on a single page at the Party Desk, where the page is available to any member who asks.

58The Card, Kept Flat

A watercolor of a small book lying perfectly flat under a clear glass panel on a bedside table, beside a folded Tether Card and a quiet lamp.

Members are advised to keep the Card flat. A book kept flat keeps its pages, its seal, and its temper; a book allowed to curl begins, in time, to resemble the condition of its owner, which the Department considers a category error. At rest, the Card should lie on a level surface, under no weight heavier than its own cover requires.

In transit the Card travels in its pocket or its envelope, flat against the citizen or flat against the post, as applicable. The advice is not a rule and carries no penalty, like much of the Party's guidance. It is simply the accumulated experience of the Party Desk, offered in the same spirit as the motto: It rises, politely. The Card, kept flat, rises with it.

59The Party Desk

A watercolor of a broad wooden desk in a calm office, with in-trays, a seal press, bound registries, and a single chair, morning light entering from a tall window.

The Party Desk is the office that receives all filings of the Party of Release. Applications, renewals, reports of loss, congress papers, and correspondence of every kind arrive at the Desk, and everything the Party issues leaves from it. The Desk is a desk. Behind it sits a clerk of the Department of Weightlessness, whose name is recorded and whose manner is procedural.

Filings are acknowledged in the order received, entered the same day where the day permits, and answered by post. The Desk keeps the Registry of the Unbound current, holds Card No. 001, and maintains the Chart of Departures for members. No filing is refused for altitude, handwriting within reason, or delay. The Desk is the ground on which the Party's records stand.

60The Congress in Writing

A watercolor of many desks in many distant quiet rooms, each with a single sheet of paper and a pen at rest, connected only by envelopes waiting in a row of letterboxes.

A congress of the Party of Release is held in writing, because the members are dispersed, at altitude, and under no obligation to gather. The Party Desk issues the agenda by post, numbered and complete. Each member considers the items in private, in silence consistent with the anthem, and returns a written response within the period stated on the agenda itself.

The responses are collected at the Desk, counted in the order received, and entered into the congress record, which is then circulated to all members by post. A decision is whatever the record says it is, and the record is the congress. No hall is booked. No photograph is taken. The congress rises, politely, from the page, and adjourns upon filing.

61The Keeper of the Tether

A solitary officer in a pale coat holding a long, slack tether that curves gently upward into a wash of silver-grey sky above a quiet lunar plain.

The Keeper of the Tether is the party officer responsible for tethers, and for knowing, politely, who holds whose line. It passes, when it passes, by a letter of transfer recorded in duplicate, one copy retained by the Party Desk and one copy left to drift in the Registry.

No tether in the Keeper's charge has ever been pulled. The duty, as minuted, is to hold without holding fast, and to release without abandoning. When a citizen of the Unbound asks whose line they are on, the Keeper answers from memory first, and from the register second, and the two answers, to date, have always agreed.

62The Office of the Keeper

A small, tidy room rendered in soft watercolour, with a single desk, a coiled tether on a hook, and a tall window opening onto empty, luminous air.

The Office of the Keeper adjoins the Party Desk, though neither office will confirm which was built first. It contains a desk, a chair that does not swivel, a hook for the ceremonial tether, a shelf for incoming letters awaiting their turn, and a window that is kept open in all weathers, there being, strictly speaking, no weather to keep out.

Visitors are received without appointment, as appointments require a shared hour and the Unbound keep their hours loosely. Business is conducted at the pace of correspondence. The Keeper offers tea, notes the request in a fair hand, and files it under the appropriate line, where it remains available for inspection by anyone patient enough to float to the cabinet.

63The Registry of the Unbound, Party Copy

An open ledger on a lectern, its pages filled with fine ruled lines and small ink figures of rising figures, bathed in pale lunar light.

The Registry of the Unbound is maintained in two copies. The departmental copy rests with the Antigravity Division, Dept of Weightlessness, beside the Certificates of Release, Form HLH-A-001, in the cabinet reserved for that purpose. The Party Copy rests with the Party Desk, and is, by agreement, identical in every particular except the handwriting, which is slightly more hopeful.

Each entry records a name, a date of release, and the number of the corresponding Party Card, Form HLH-P-001, where one has been issued. Entries are never struck through. A citizen who returns to weight is not removed; a marginal note is added, in pencil, recording that the citizen is presently elsewhere, and the line is left respectfully blank. The Party Copy has one public page: the party's digital Hall of Fame, where the profile of every member who writes to the Desk — name, number, and date of filing — is placed on the record and kept there, in writing, like everything the party keeps. It admits no portraits and permits no removal; fame, in the party's keeping, is a filing that has been answered.

64The Quorum of the Air

A wide, faint circle of small drifting figures suspended at different heights over a calm grey landscape, connected by almost invisible threads of light.

A quorum of The Party of Release is not counted by heads, heads being difficult to align at a single level. It is counted by presence acknowledged in writing. When the required number of members have replied to a summons, in whatever order and from whatever altitude, the Congress in Writing is declared quorate and business may proceed.

The figure required is recorded in the standing orders and has not changed since the founding on paper in MMXXVI. This stability is not attributed to wisdom, but to the difficulty of amending a number that everyone has already memorised. The Keeper attests the count. The Desk files the attestation. The air, consulted, raises no objection.

65Committees That Correspond

Several small desks arranged far apart across a misty plain, with paper sheets travelling slowly between them on fine, sagging lines.

The Party of Release conducts its committee work entirely by correspondence. Committees do not meet, in the seated sense; they correspond, which the standing orders treat as meeting conducted over a longer and more considerate interval. Each committee has a chairman, a secretary, and a member charged with waiting, whose duties are recognised as substantial.

Minutes are circulated before decisions are taken, so that disagreement may arrive early and be accommodated politely. A committee that reaches unanimity too quickly is asked to correspond again, on the principle that a matter understood by everyone at once has probably not yet been understood. Reports, when complete, are brief, signed, and filed without ceremony.

66The Archive of Departures

Tall, narrow shelves receding into soft shadow, each shelf holding neat bundles of tied papers, with a single shaft of pale light falling from above.

The Archive of Departures preserves the record of every release acknowledged by the party: the certificate, the card, the letter of notification, and, where supplied, a short account by the citizen of the moment of rising. These accounts vary greatly in length. One, much cited by the archivists, consists of the single word, filed without comment.

Nothing in the Archive is permitted to settle permanently. At intervals the archivist passes through the shelves and lifts each bundle a little, so that the collection remains, in the technical sense, airborne. Researchers may consult any file on application to the Party Desk. Copies are made by hand, slowly, which is considered part of the research.

67Elections, Held by Letter

A procession of sealed envelopes drifting in a loose, orderly stream across a pale sky toward a small distant desk on a hill.

Elections in The Party of Release are held by letter, there being no hall that could contain the electorate at a common level. Ballot papers are issued by the Party Desk, marked at leisure, and returned by post. The count begins when the returning officer judges that sufficient time has elapsed for even the most distant reply to have had its chance.

Results are announced in writing, in the same envelope size as the summons, so that victory and defeat arrive in identical stationery. No candidate has ever campaigned in person. A short statement of intention, not exceeding one page, is permitted, and most candidates, out of courtesy to the count, submit considerably less than the permitted page.

68The Chair, Rotating Slowly

A single empty chair turning almost imperceptibly above a polished floor, its shadow moving in a slow circle in muted blue and grey washes.

The presiding office of the Congress in Writing is known simply as the Chair, and it rotates slowly. The rotation is literal in the ceremonial portrait and figurative in the standing orders, a distinction the party has never found it necessary to resolve. Each holder serves for one correspondence, however long that correspondence takes to complete its full and unhurried course.

The Chair does not direct debate, there being no debate to direct, but acknowledges receipt of all contributions in an even hand. At the close of a congress the Chair signs the record last, after every other signature has settled. The motto is then read, if read is the word for a held silence: It rises, politely.

69Motions, Seconded by Post

Two paper sheets meeting mid-air above a quiet desk, their corners almost touching, against a background of soft, layered grey hills.

A motion before the Congress in Writing requires a proposer, a seconder, and a legible text. The proposer submits by post. The seconder, who may be at any distance, seconds by post in turn. Until the second envelope is opened and recorded by the Party Desk, the motion is described, accurately, as being in transit.

Motions are rarely lost. They are, more often, overtaken by the events they were intended to address, and are then withdrawn with thanks. Withdrawn motions are not discarded; they are filed in the Archive of Departures under a separate heading, on the reasoning that an intention, once properly seconded, has also departed somewhere worth recording.

70The Party Press

A lone postman in muted colours carrying a neat stack of newsletters across an empty, airy plain toward a scatter of distant, floating dwellings.

The party press consists of one newsletter, issued when there is sufficient news and sufficient paper. It carries the record of congresses, the names of new citizens of the Unbound, selected correspondence, and, on the final page, the flag of the party, which is a chart of departures, redrawn each issue to include the latest.

There is no wind to carry it, so it is mailed. Subscribers receive their copies at irregular intervals, which the editors describe as the natural frequency of events. Back numbers are available from the Party Desk. The anthem, a held silence, is printed at the head of the first page as a blank measure, to be observed before reading.

71Discipline: A Polite Reminder

A small sealed note resting on an otherwise empty table, beside a glass of water that casts a long, calm shadow in the afternoon light.

Discipline in The Party of Release takes a single form: a polite reminder. It is issued in writing, by the Party Desk, on the advice of the Keeper of the Tether, and it states the matter plainly, once, without emphasis. No second reminder follows in haste; the party allows a full correspondence for the first to be considered.

The offences contemplated are correspondingly modest: a tether left unrecorded, a motion seconded before it was read, a silence held ostentatiously during the anthem. Most reminders are answered with an apology of equal brevity, which is filed with the reminder itself, and the pair are thereafter treated, by common consent, as a closed and instructive document.

72Expulsion, and Why It Has Never Happened

An untouched, neatly folded form lying alone in an open drawer, dustless and pale, beneath a soft wash of grey-blue light.

The standing orders provide for expulsion, and the necessary form exists, printed, numbered, and kept in a drawer of the Party Desk. It has never been used. The committee charged with reviewing the procedure reports, at intervals, that the form remains in good condition and that no circumstances have yet arisen to justify its removal from the drawer.

The difficulty, as minuted, is practical as well as principled. To expel a citizen of the Unbound, the party would first have to establish where, in the relevant sense, the citizen is, and then deliver the notice by post to that address. Citizens proposed for expulsion have invariably, by the time of delivery, become examples of the very freedom the party exists to record.

73The Loyal Opposition of the Downward

A sturdy desk anchored firmly on a dark lunar plain, facing upward toward faint drifting shapes, with a neatly stacked pile of heavy-looking ledgers beside it.

The loyal opposition of The Party of Release is the Gravity Division, Dept of Lunar Construction, and its loyalty has never been in question. It opposes, in principle, the direction of travel. Where the party records a release, the Division records a plot. Where the party issues a card, the Division issues a permit, and files both facts without resentment.

This rivalry is institutional, courteous, and complete. The Division maintains, in its paperwork for Mare Tranquillitatis Sector G, that what rises must be accounted for by what remains below. The party maintains that what remains below is, chiefly, the paperwork. Both positions are entered in the minutes, and both are, within the Holding, entirely respectable.

74Correspondence with the Gravity Division

Two streams of envelopes crossing in opposite directions between a low stone building and a pale tower, over a still, silvery landscape.

Correspondence between the party and the Gravity Division is regular, formal, and slow, by mutual preference. Letters are addressed in full, signed in full, and answered in the order in which they were received, even when a later letter plainly answers an earlier one. The file, read in sequence, therefore contains several apologies for delays that had not yet occurred.

No letter from either desk has ever been marked urgent. The Division, working at lunar gravity of 1.62 m/s², regards urgency as a local condition. The party, working without weight, regards it as a claim requiring evidence. Between these two views a steady, useful correspondence has continued since the founding of Hi-Tech Limited Holding in MMXXVI.

75Two Desks, One Holding

Two desks seen from above in a shared hall, one weighted with stones and ledgers, the other light and almost lifting at its corners, under even pale light.

The Party Desk and the desk of the Gravity Division stand, on paper, in the same hall of Hi-Tech Limited Holding. In practice each administration describes the hall differently: the Division by its floor plan and load-bearing columns, the party by the quality of the air near the ceiling. 81The Party's Position on Falling

A watercolor of a registrar's desk beside a shaft of pale light, where a feather and a stone rest side by side on an empty blotter.

The Party of Release records its position on falling without hostility and without haste. Falling is understood as a direction taken under instruction, often repeatedly, and usually toward ground prepared in advance. The Party does not dispute the usefulness of descent for building, landing, and ordinary return. It observes only that a citizen who has ceased to fall has not thereby erred, and should not be corrected by argument or by rope.

Accordingly, the Party maintains no campaign against falling and issues no literature to floors. Members who fall are received without remark and filed under temporary return. 85Ceremonies of the Party

Outsiders sometimes ask whether so plain a programme can properly be called ceremonial. The Desk answers that ceremony is not measured by volume. 88The Held Silence

A watercolor of a small assembly standing perfectly still in a pale hall, heads slightly raised, as dust motes hang motionless in the light.

The anthem of the Party of Release is a held silence, performed standing, seated, or at altitude, according to the condition of those present. It begins when the Keeper of the Tether lowers one hand. It ends when the Keeper raises it again. Between those two gestures, nothing is required of anyone. Breathing is permitted and, in most cases, continues. The silence is not empty. It is, the Party maintains, fully occupied.

New members occasionally ask how long the anthem lasts, and the Desk answers honestly that this has never been timed. Estimates in the minutes range from a held breath to a long afternoon. What matters is that the silence is held in common, the way a tether is held: not tightly, not carelessly, and by more than one pair of hands. When the hand rises, ordinary sound resumes, and the clerk notes simply that the anthem was performed.

89The Reading of the Chart

The flag of the Party of Release is a chart of departures, and at appointed ceremonies it is read rather than saluted. The clerk unrolls it to the current month and reads the departures in order: name, date, and direction, where a direction was given. Many entries list no direction at all, which the clerk reads as a pause, evenly, without emphasis. The audience listens the way one listens to weather in another country.

The chart is not a map, and the Party is careful never to call it one. A map implies that someone might follow. The chart records only that citizens left, in good order, on dates that can be verified, toward destinations that remain their own affair. When the reading ends, the chart is rolled from the bottom upward, so that the earliest departures are always the last to disappear from view.

113Can the Party Expel Me?

The Desk answers as follows. The Party of Release has no procedure for expulsion, and the Congress in Writing has never been asked to invent one. A registry that records release is poorly suited to recording banishment, and the Desk has consistently declined to develop the necessary forms. What the Desk can do, and does, is correct the record. An entry made in error will be amended, an entry made twice will be consolidated, and an entry made in another citizen's name will be removed and refiled properly. 118Collectible Print

What exists of the Party of Release, beyond the imagining, is print: pages, cards, and plates, made to be kept.
