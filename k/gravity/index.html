01Gravity

The Cavendish apparatus of 1797–98 — a torsion balance in a closed case, weighing the attraction between lead spheres

Gravity is one of the four fundamental interactions of nature, alongside electromagnetism and the strong and weak nuclear forces. It is the attraction that draws mass and energy toward one another, holding planets in orbit, stars together, and feet on the ground. In Newton's account it is a force acting between masses. In Einstein's general theory of relativity, completed in 1915, it is not a force in the ordinary sense: mass and energy curve spacetime, and objects follow the straightest paths available in that curved geometry. Both descriptions remain in use — Newton's for most engineering, Einstein's wherever precision or extremes demand it.

Newton published universal gravitation in the Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica in 1687. Every mass attracts every other mass with a force proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The claim was radical in its economy: one law governed the falling apple, the Moon, the tides, and the planets. Newton predicted orbital motion with unprecedented accuracy, but offered no mechanism for how attraction acted across empty space, writing that he framed no hypotheses. That unanswered how stood for more than two centuries.

Measuring gravity's strength took longer than stating its law. In 1797–98 Henry Cavendish used a delicate torsion balance, devised by John Michell, to measure the faint attraction between lead spheres. The experiment — often described as weighing the Earth — gave the first laboratory value for the gravitational constant, G. More than two centuries later G, now 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², remains the least precisely known fundamental constant. Independent modern measurements still disagree beyond their stated uncertainties, a reminder of how feebly gravity couples to laboratory apparatus.

Einstein replaced action at a distance with geometry. General relativity, presented in 1915, describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime produced by mass-energy: matter tells spacetime how to curve, and curved spacetime tells matter how to move. The theory explained a long-standing anomaly — the perihelion of Mercury advances by 43 arcseconds per century more than Newtonian gravity predicts. In May 1919, expeditions led by Arthur Eddington and Frank Dyson photographed stars near the eclipsed Sun and found their light deflected by the predicted amount. Redshift, signal time delay, and binary pulsars have confirmed the theory wherever it has been checked.

At Earth's surface the acceleration due to gravity, g, is about 9.8 m/s². A conventional exact value, standard gravity, was fixed in 1901 at 9.80665 m/s² and is still used to define units and calibrate instruments. The measured value varies: about 9.78 m/s² at the equator and 9.83 m/s² at the poles, because Earth rotates and bulges, and it falls with altitude and varies locally with geology. On the Moon surface gravity is 1.62 m/s², roughly one sixth of Earth's; on Mars it is 3.71 m/s². These numbers set the weight of every structure, body, and payload on each world.

General relativity predicted in 1916 that accelerating masses radiate gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime itself. In 1974 Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor discovered a binary pulsar whose orbit is shrinking at exactly the rate expected if the system loses energy to gravitational waves; the work earned the Nobel Prize in 1993. Direct detection waited until 14 September 2015, when the twin LIGO observatories recorded GW150914, the merger of two black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses some 1.3 billion light-years away. LIGO, Virgo, and KAGRA have since catalogued scores of mergers, and gravitational-wave astronomy is now a routine observational science.

At cosmic scales gravity is the architect. It gathers gas into stars and galaxies, holds clusters together, drives large-scale structure, and in its extreme form produces black holes from which light cannot escape. It also raises the tides through the Moon's differential pull on Earth's oceans. The same gravity is a working tool. Satellite orbits are Newtonian and relativistic bookkeeping; the Global Positioning System must correct its clocks for both special and general relativity, a net effect of about 38 microseconds per day that would otherwise accumulate into position errors of roughly 10 km daily. Gravimeters measuring local g to parts in a billion support geodesy, resource exploration, and monitoring of aquifers and volcanoes.

For all its successes, the theory is incomplete. General relativity and quantum mechanics have not been reconciled into a tested theory of quantum gravity; string theory and loop quantum gravity remain without experimental confirmation. The rotation of galaxies and the dynamics of clusters imply far more gravitating mass than can be seen — the dark-matter problem, unsolved since the 1930s — while the accelerating expansion of the universe, discovered in 1998, is attributed to an equally unexplained dark energy. Gravity is at once the best-measured everyday phenomenon and the interaction whose deepest nature is least understood.

This page begins from that established science, stated as plainly as it can be stated. What follows is different in kind: the program of the people who support working on gravity itself — not merely using it or observing it, but trying to reduce, shield, or reverse it. Their approaches, the evidence offered for them, and the verdicts the evidence has earned are set out in the halls that follow, honestly labeled, so a reader can always tell what is known, what is hoped, and what has already been tested and found wanting.

02Gravity Is a Problem

A rocket on its pad, a tower crane, and a human figure — three different payers of the same gravity tax

Most sciences describe what exists. This page begins with a complaint. Gravity, for the engineer, is not a wonder but a cost — a continuous charge levied on almost everything human beings build, move, or carry, including their own bodies. Every structure must be strong enough to stand in it. Every aircraft must spend energy, every minute of flight, opposing it. Every rocket must lift not only its payload but the fuel needed to lift the fuel.

Call it the gravity tax. A building pays it in steel and concrete. A body pays it in worn joints, a heart working against a column of blood, and falls — a leading cause of injury and death in old age. A space program pays it most visibly: low Earth orbit is only a few hundred kilometres up, yet reaching it demands kilometres per second bought against a 9.8 m/s² headwind.

Engineers normally treat the tax as fixed. They design around it, lighten structures, improve engines, and accept the rate as a law of the site, like weather. That acceptance is rational; two centuries of work have produced no way to change the rate itself.

This page exists because some people — a small, persistent minority of physicists, engineers, financiers, and enthusiasts — propose to work on the tax itself. Not to oppose gravity more efficiently, as wings and rockets do, but to reduce the charge at its source. The proposal may be wrong. The halls that follow test it claim by claim. But the problem it answers is real, measurable, and paid by everyone, every day.

03The Terms of This Page

A page that supports a research program owes its reader a charter before it asks for sympathy. Every substantive claim in the halls ahead carries one of four labels, applied to the individual approach, not to the hopes attached to it. The labels are the page's contract, and they do not soften as the story becomes more exciting.

Established means the effect is real, replicated, and explained by accepted physics — ion wind, diamagnetic levitation, and light pressure live here, however they were once advertised. Speculative means the idea is not yet inconsistent with known physics, but has no confirming experiment. Unconfirmed means a positive result has been reported but no independent laboratory has reproduced it under controlled conditions. Disproven means the claim was tested properly, the effect vanished or was explained, and the matter is closed unless genuinely new evidence appears.

One rule governs the whole page without exception: no device anywhere on it is claimed to work. Not the historical machines, not the laboratory anomalies, not the modern prototypes. Where a researcher or company claims otherwise, the claim is reported as a claim, with its date and its test history.

The charter also promises a conclusion. Block 110 will carry a single dated verdict sentence, written to be quoted out of context without misleading anyone. Everything between here and there — the history, the physics, the experiments — is the evidence that sentence must be able to stand on.

04What the Word Must Mean

Three arrows distinguished — weight reduced, weight opposed by lift, and weight left untouched by thrust

Antigravity is a word ruined by loose use, so this page defines it strictly before using it again. Antigravity would mean reducing, shielding, or reversing weight itself: an object that, sitting still on a balance, registers less than its mass and the local value of g require — or nothing, or less than nothing. The object has not been pushed up. The pull on it has changed.

That definition excludes almost everything that flies. Levitation opposes weight with another force: a magnet repels, a frog floats in a strong magnetic field, a helicopter pushes air down, a rocket throws mass behind it. These are propulsion and support — real, useful, and fully understood. In every case the air, the exhaust, or the field accounts for the full weight. Nothing has been shielded; the bill has been paid by a different payer.

The distinction matters because most demonstrations offered as antigravity are, on inspection, levitation or thrust in costume. Each must answer one question before any other: where did the reaction go? If air moved, ions fled, or a field pushed against the laboratory, weight was opposed, not reduced.

Held to the strict definition, antigravity remains a precise and testable idea — which is why it can be investigated honestly. A mass losing weight, by itself, in still conditions, would be unmistakable. Nothing in the halls that follow has yet met that standard, but the standard itself separates a research program from a magic show.

059.8

A ladder of energies — the rungs from a standing jump to orbit to escape, each priced in kilojoules per kilogram

Two numbers govern every space program ever flown. The first is 9.8: the acceleration, in metres per second squared, with which Earth pulls everything at its surface back down. It sounds modest, but it never pauses, never tires, and applies to every kilogram for every second of an ascent.

The second number is 11.2: escape velocity, in kilometres per second, the speed an unpowered object at Earth's surface would need to leave and never return. Orbit is only slightly cheaper — about 7.8 km/s for low Earth orbit, before drag and gravity losses push the practical budget above 9 km/s. No chemical reaction releases energy fast enough to make those speeds easy, which is why rockets are built as they are.

The cost is not metaphorical. Of the roughly 2,970 tonnes of a Saturn V on its pad, the Apollo missions returned a command module of under 6 tonnes; the rest was structure and propellant spent buying speed against 9.8. Modern vehicles improve the fraction but not the order of magnitude. A kilogram in low orbit still represents on the order of 3 × 10⁷ joules of kinetic energy alone, delivered in minutes without damaging its contents.

Supporters of gravity research return to these numbers the way economists return to a tax rate. 9.8 sets the rate; 11.2 sets the price of leaving altogether. Every launch provider and every plan for a presence beyond Earth is an answer to them. The program on this page is simply the refusal to assume the numbers themselves are beyond negotiation.

06The Rocket Equation's Verdict

A rocket drawn as nested fractions — payload inside structure inside propellant, the equation made visible

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky published the governing arithmetic of rocketry in 1903, and it has not relented since. The rocket equation states that a rocket's change in velocity equals its exhaust velocity multiplied by the natural logarithm of the ratio between its starting mass and its final mass. Velocity grows only logarithmically with fuel; fuel, meanwhile, must itself be lifted, and the fuel to lift that fuel must be lifted too.

The consequence is the familiar silhouette of every launcher: a tower of propellant carrying a comparatively small vehicle carrying a very small payload. To double velocity change at fixed exhaust velocity does not double the fuel — it squares the mass ratio required. Chemical exhaust velocities, capped by the energy in chemical bonds at roughly 4.5 km/s for the best hydrogen–oxygen engines, sit uncomfortably close to the 9 km/s or more that reaching orbit demands. The margin is bought with staging, discarding empty tanks on the way up.

Engineers have spent a century improving everything around the equation — materials, pumps, guidance, reusability. None of it touches the equation itself, which is not a technology but a consequence of conservation of momentum for any vehicle that pushes itself by throwing mass backward.

That is why the supporters in this story dream past the equation rather than through it. A vehicle whose weight were reduced, or which could push against the gravitational field itself rather than against its own exhaust, would need no better rocket. It would need no rocket at all. The dream is understandable. Whether nature permits it is the question the rest of this page is for.

07The Weakest Force

Gravity's most disorienting property is how weak it is. A small refrigerator magnet holds a paperclip against the pull of the entire Earth — all 6 × 10²⁴ kilograms of it — and the magnet wins without effort. The gravitational attraction between two protons is roughly 10³⁶ times weaker than their electrical repulsion, and gravity's coupling is commonly described as some 10⁴⁰ times weaker than electromagnetism's at everyday scales. The hierarchy between gravity's strength and the other forces remains one of physics' unexplained numbers.

This weakness cuts in a surprising direction. The energy needed to lift things is trivial: raising 1 kg by 1 m costs mgh, just under 10 joules — less than a phone battery stores by a factor of thousands. If antigravity were a problem of energy supply, it would have been solved by a car battery. No supporter with a notebook believes the obstacle is power.

The obstacle is grip. Electromagnetism can be worked on because charges come in two signs, can be gathered, shielded by conductors, and switched. Gravity, as far as any experiment has found, couples only to mass-energy, which comes in one sign, cannot be screened by any material, and cannot be cancelled by arrangement. There is no gravitational conductor, no insulator, and no negative mass in hand to build a circuit with.

Every approach in the halls ahead is, at bottom, a proposed handle: a material, a field, a rotation, a quantum effect that might give a first grip on an interaction that otherwise offers none. The repeated failure is informative — it maps how completely gravity refuses the tricks that work on every other force.

08The Equivalence Principle

A drop tower drawn as a wall — two masses of different composition falling together, side by side, all the way down

At the foundation of general relativity sits a simple observation: everything falls the same way. A hammer and a feather, dropped on the Moon by Apollo 15 commander David Scott in 1971, struck the dust together. Inertial mass — resistance to being pushed — and gravitational mass — susceptibility to being pulled — are, in every test, the same quantity. Einstein called their equivalence the happiest thought of his life, because a gravity that accelerates all materials identically can be redescribed as geometry.

The tests have become extraordinarily fine. The French CNES satellite MICROSCOPE, launched in 2016 and reporting final results in 2022, compared the free fall of platinum and titanium test masses in orbit and found them equivalent to about 1 part in 10¹⁵. Torsion balances and lunar laser ranging, which watches Earth and Moon fall toward the Sun together, reach comparable precision. No dependence on composition, temperature, or structure has ever been confirmed.

For antigravity, this is the wall. A shield that worked on one material and not another would break the equivalence principle at a level experiment would long ago have noticed. A shield that worked on everything equally must intervene in spacetime itself. Any proposal must therefore claim that the best-tested symmetry in physics fails somewhere specific — at some field strength, frequency, or configuration not yet examined.

That is not a logical impossibility; symmetries have broken before, and each generation of equivalence tests has been worth doing precisely because a violation would be revolutionary. But it sets the price of admission. A gravity shield is a claim against 10⁻¹⁵, and the halls ahead should be read with that number in mind.

09The Shield Paradox

A wheel that cannot turn — a mass lifted behind a shield and dropped outside it, the loop priced in joules

Before any shielding experiment is discussed, a thought experiment prices the claim. Suppose a true gravity shield exists: a plate above which gravity is reduced or absent. Lift a mass behind the shield, where lifting is cheap or free. Move it sideways, out from behind the shield, at no cost in height. Then let it fall in full gravity, driving a generator on the way down, and return it to the start. The cycle yields net energy from nothing, indefinitely.

The argument is old, simple, and has never been answered by any shielding proposal. It does not prove that partial effects are impossible — a shield that consumed more energy than the cycle returned would break no law. What the paradox establishes is that a passive, free shield is not a machine but a perpetual-motion claim, and it inherits the entire burden of proof that perpetual motion has accumulated over centuries of failed wheels and weights.

This is why serious researchers, including NASA's own program in the 1990s, framed their goals carefully: not free shielding, but any measurable coupling — a force, a weight change, an anomaly — whose energy accounts could be audited line by line. The paradox does not forbid discovery. It forbids a certain kind of bookkeeping error, historically the kind most often made.

Keep the wheel in mind through Hall III. Whenever a device is said to rise, hover, or lighten without an obvious payer — current drawn, air moved, propellant spent, heat dumped — the shield paradox asks the only question that matters: show the full cycle, with numbers, and point to where the energy came from. Claims that survive that question deserve laboratories.

10What Would Count

A calibrated balance in a vacuum chamber — the apparatus a successful claim must one day face

A field with a century of false alarms survives, if it survives at all, by fixing its success criterion in advance and refusing to move it. This page fixes it here, before any device is described, so no later enthusiasm can quietly lower the bar. The criterion is deliberately ordinary; that is its strength.

Success would be: a mass loses weight. Not a device that rises, jumps, drifts, or vibrates — a mass, on a calibrated balance, registering less than its established weight. The test runs in vacuum, to remove air currents, ion wind, and thermal convection, the three great impersonators of this field. The apparatus sits on a quiet mount, its power metered, its temperature logged, and every cable and field that could exert a conventional force accounted for in a published error budget.

Two further conditions separate a result from an anecdote. The effect must reverse, scale, or switch with the claimed cause — turn the device off and the weight returns — and it must do so in a laboratory that did not build the device, from published instructions, on apparatus the independent team controls. One laboratory's anomaly is a research problem. Two laboratories' agreement is a discovery.

Nothing described on this page has yet met this criterion. Several claims have met parts of it, and those partial cases — the ones that reached real balances in real vacuum chambers — are the most instructive stories in the halls ahead. When a claim fails, the page will say which condition it failed.

11Why Support It Anyway

A small laboratory bench beside a launch pad — the cheap test and the expensive dream in one frame

Given the equivalence principle, the shield paradox, and a century without a qualifying result, a fair question follows: why support this research at all? The supporter's case, at its strongest and most honest, rests on three legs — none of which is a claim that a device already works.

First, the payoff is genuinely unbounded. A controllable coupling to gravity would not improve transportation; it would replace its foundations, from launch vehicles to civil engineering to medicine's long war with falls and immobility. In expected-value terms, even a small probability on an outcome of that size can justify a small, bounded expenditure — provided the expenditure stays small and the accounting stays honest. The case is for a lottery ticket bought openly, not a mortgage taken out on the prize.

Second, the search has repeatedly produced real physics on its way to failing. Equivalence-principle tests mounted partly to close the door on shielding claims became precision instruments in their own right. High-voltage thrust research clarified ion propulsion. Superconductivity experiments run to chase weight anomalies sharpened gravimetry. A well-run negative search is not wasted money; it is how several respectable subfields received their best instruments.

Third, and simplest: a cheap test beats a confident dismissal. Most historical claims in this field could be, and eventually were, settled on a bench for less than the cost of the conferences held to denounce them. Support, on this page, means funding the test, publishing the result either way, and keeping the labels honest. The halls that follow are the record of what that support has bought so far.

12Cavorite: The Fiction That Started It

A timeline in one line — 1901, a fictional shutter of Cavorite opening toward the Moon

The antigravity program begins, as several technologies have, in fiction. H. G. Wells published The First Men in the Moon in 1901, fourteen years before general relativity, and equipped his inventor, Mr Cavor, with a substance fitted precisely to this page's strict definition: Cavorite, a material opaque to gravity as a shutter is opaque to light. Raised or lowered like blinds, it shields whatever sits above it, and a spherical vessel lined with Cavorite shutters sails to the Moon by selective weightlessness.

Wells understood the idea's appeal exactly. His narrator is a businessman who grasps instantly that a gravity shutter is worth more than any mine; Cavor, the distracted physicist, grasps none of it. The novel is a thought experiment about value: whoever controls weight controls transport, war, and trade. Every later pitch in this history, from 1950s trade journalism to modern startup decks, replays the conversation between Wells's two characters.

Fiction's role deserves neither embarrassment nor exaggeration. Cavorite solved, on paper, a problem physics had not yet fully stated — the equivalence principle was six years from Einstein's happiest thought, and the shield paradox is nowhere in the novel. Wells could imagine a shutter because nothing yet known forbade one quite so specifically. Later writers inherited the device after the prohibitions were known, which makes their versions nostalgia rather than speculation.

What 1901 started was the audience. Generations of engineers first met gravity shielding as readers, and some carried the image into laboratories and boardrooms. Hall II is the story of that contact: an idea born in a novel, institutionalised by a financier, industrialised by a boom, and tested ever since.

13The Gravity Research Foundation

Babson's institute in New Boston, New Hampshire — memorial stones and an essay prize, the movement's first address

The movement's first institution was founded not by a physicist but by a financier who hated gravity personally. Roger W. Babson, founder of Babson College and a nationally known economic forecaster, blamed gravity for drownings in his family — his elder sister Edith had drowned in 1903 — and spoke of the force as an enemy that might, with research, be beaten. In 1948 he established the Gravity Research Foundation to encourage work toward reducing the effects of gravity.

Babson's money was real and his methods were those of his era. The foundation ran an annual essay competition on gravitation, and over the decades that competition attracted entries from physicists of genuine standing — won at various times by researchers including Stephen Hawking, Roger Penrose, and George Smoot, for orthodox work on gravitation rather than shielding. An institute and memorial site at New Boston, New Hampshire, gave the enterprise a physical home.

The result was a productive contradiction. Babson funded the foundation to defeat gravity, and its most durable contribution was to mainstream gravitational physics: essays, conferences, and steady legitimacy for a subject then considered a backwater of relativity. The shielding research he hoped for produced nothing that survived testing — a pattern the rest of this hall repeats at larger scales and budgets.

Babson matters because he set the supporter's template: private money, offered openly, for an unfashionable question, with the results left to speak. He was eccentric and he was sincere, and the institution outlived the mockery of his gravity rhetoric. Every later patron in this story would face his question in a harder form: how to fund the test without endorsing the answer.

14The 1950s Boom

A row of postwar aerospace letterheads — Martin, Bell, Lear, Goodyear — each with gravity listed among its interests

For roughly a decade after the Second World War, antigravity was not a fringe topic but a line item. American aerospace, flush with defence contracts and racing through the jet and rocket revolutions in a single generation, saw no reason the next revolution should not be gravitational. By the mid-1950s, trade and popular press were reporting gravity-control research — the 'G-projects' — at firms and institutes across the industry.

The names were substantial. The Glenn L. Martin Company, Bell Aircraft, Lear, and Goodyear were among the firms reported to list gravity research among their interests. Journalist Ansel Talbert's widely syndicated 1955 articles described a coordinated effort and quoted researchers predicting gravity-controlled aircraft within a generation. For a few years, a young physicist could work on gravitation's applications without apologising for the topic.

Context explains the optimism better than credulity does. Between 1945 and 1955, fission, jet propulsion, radar, transistors, and orbital rocketry all moved from speculation to hardware; a manager who had watched four impossible things become routine could be forgiven a fifth. What the boom lacked was not intelligence but a phenomenon: unlike fission, which began with measurable neutron counts, the G-projects began with a definition, a hope, and a great deal of vagueness about results.

No device, memorandum, or test report from the boom years has ever surfaced that meets even the loosest version of this page's criterion. The programs left articles, organisational charts, and confidence — and then they left. The boom remains the field's central cautionary exhibit: institutional seriousness is not evidence. Only the balance is evidence.

15Townsend Brown and Electrogravitics

Brown's asymmetric capacitor — a wire, a foil, and a high-voltage supply, thrusting toward its smaller electrode

Thomas Townsend Brown was an American experimenter who, from the 1920s onward, reported that high-voltage capacitors experience a force toward their positive electrode — an effect developed with his former teacher Paul Biefeld and known as the Biefeld–Brown effect. Through the 1940s and 1950s Brown promoted it under a grander name, electrogravitics, proposing a coupling between electricity and gravity, and demonstrated devices that strained against threads, rotated on arms, and appeared to pull themselves across benches.

The thrust was real. That is what made Brown the decade's central confusion rather than a simple fraud. An asymmetric capacitor at tens of thousands of volts does experience a measurable force, strong enough to lift lightweight structures — the modern 'lifter' tradition descends directly from his devices. Demonstrators and journalists watched the apparatus move and concluded, reasonably but wrongly, that a new principle was at work. If it moved, with no propeller or jet visible, gravity seemed the only candidate left.

The explanation, established by careful tests including later vacuum-chamber work, is ion wind. The high voltage ionises surrounding air; the ions accelerate in the electric field, collide with neutral molecules, and stream away, pushing the device by reaction — an invisible rocket exhaust made of the room itself. In vacuum, where there is no air to ionise, Biefeld–Brown thrust falls to nothing or to levels consistent with residual artefacts.

Brown's legacy is therefore double. He built the template for a century of misread demonstrations — real motion, wrong ledger — and, inadvertently, an excellent teaching instrument. Electrogravitics as a theory of gravity is disproven. The little thrust that inspired it continues, correctly labelled, in ion-propulsion research.

16The Quiet Decades

By the early 1960s the institutional boom ended, and it ended without scandal, exposure, or a decisive experiment. The programs faded the way unfunded fashions do: no results had arrived to justify the next contract, jets and rockets were delivering revolutions on schedule, and the word 'antigravity' began to cost more credibility in a grant application than it could return. Firms quietly dropped the line item. Nothing had ever quite taken off, so there was no crash site.

The work did not stop; it moved. Through the 1970s, 1980s, and 1990s, speculative gravity research lived in garages, small newsletters, mail-order plans, and conference rooms at the edges of established conferences. Cut off from aerospace budgets and university laboratories, this culture kept the question alive at bench scale for three decades, largely unobserved by the institutions that had abandoned it.

What that culture got right deserves recording. It persisted, which institutions had not. It built things — thousands of replicas, variants, and test rigs — and preserved, in working form, demonstrations and questions that textbooks had dropped. Its bench skills were often considerable, and its suspicion that official science can dismiss too quickly was not always misplaced.

What it got wrong was measurement discipline, and the error compounded. Without vacuum chambers, calibrated balances, blinded controls, or published error budgets, the garage literature could not separate its rare real effects — ion wind, thermal drift, vibration, magnetic coupling to a building's steel — from the weight change it sought. Claims accumulated faster than tests could retire them, and the field carried a forty-year backlog of unpriced assertions into a medium that rewards assertion most of all.

17NASA Tries Again: Breakthrough Propulsion Physics

The programme's three goals on one chart — no propellant, no light-speed limit, new energy — under strict rules of evidence

In 1996 the question returned to an institution, on deliberately unglamorous terms. NASA established the Breakthrough Propulsion Physics programme — BPP — at its Glenn Research Center in Ohio, managed by aerospace engineer Marc Millis. Its premise was administrative rather than romantic: interstellar flight is impossible under current propulsion physics, so a small, rigorous effort would assess whether any published claim or theoretical opening suggested physics from which a breakthrough could come.

The scale was modest and the accounting public. From 1996 to 2002, BPP spent about $1.2 million, distributed across peer-reviewed workshops, a small number of competitively selected research tasks, and assessments of the literature. Its three stated goals were precise: discover propulsion that requires no propellant mass, discover methods to exceed or circumvent the light-speed limit, and discover new energy methods to power such propulsion. Nothing in the charter promised any of the three existed.

What distinguished BPP was its etiquette. Claims were welcome from any source, including the garage literature, but each had to arrive as a testable experiment with an error budget, and null results were published with the same care as positive ones would have been. Millis insisted the product was not a drive but a method: a reliable way to tell a genuine anomaly from the long catalogue of artefacts this field produces.

BPP is the hinge of this hall. Before it, the field's history is patrons, firms, and enthusiasts. After it, every serious claim would be measured, knowingly or not, against BPP's standard of conduct. The programme closed in 2002 — why it closed, and why the honest answer is more useful than the conspiratorial one, is the next block's subject.

18Why BPP Closed

A triage chart — many claims assessed, none carried forward, the file closed by budget rather than by verdict of impossibility

Breakthrough Propulsion Physics ended in 2002 when its funding was not renewed, amid tightening NASA budgets and shifting agency priorities. In later retelling the closure is sometimes cast as suppression — a breakthrough found and buried. The programme's own reports, which remain public, record something plainer and more valuable: no breakthrough had occurred. Triage, not conspiracy, closed the file.

Triage is the correct word. BPP's assessments worked through the era's candidate claims — including superconductor weight-anomaly reports examined in Hall IV — and found effects that shrank as measurement improved, artefacts with conventional explanations, or experiments too loosely reported to test at all. A programme funded to find a qualifying anomaly, which finds none in six years, has done its job and simultaneously undermined its case for a seventh year. Millis said as much, in print, while the programme was still running.

The closure therefore carries information in both directions. It is not evidence that breakthrough propulsion is impossible; a $1.2 million survey could not establish that, and BPP never claimed it could. It is evidence about base rates — about how the specific claims available in 1996–2002 performed under the best test etiquette the field has produced.

BPP's lasting bequest is procedural. Its insistence on pre-stated criteria, published nulls, and error budgets before enthusiasm became the etiquette every later serious effort — including this page's four labels and fixed success criterion — consciously follows. The programme bought no propulsion. It bought the field its conscience, at $1.2 million.

19Project Greenglow

A blue-sky file open on a British engineer's desk — gravity control examined, noted, and shelved

Britain's entry in this history is smaller, later, and understated. Project Greenglow was a blue-sky research effort within BAE Systems, the British aerospace and defence company, publicly acknowledged around the turn of the century and associated with engineer Ron Evans, who had pursued gravitational questions since his student years. Where the 1950s American boom spoke of imminent product, Greenglow spoke the language of the file review: examine the physics, survey the claims, and record what a serious firm should keep watching.

The study's remit covered this hall's familiar territory — the theoretical standing of gravity modification under general relativity, the reported anomalies then circulating, and whether any laboratory effect warranted company investment. Its researchers consulted academic specialists and treated the equivalence principle and energy arguments of Hall I as the constraints they were. Nothing in Greenglow's public record suggests classified triumph or suppressed hardware; the record suggests homework, done properly.

The outcome matched the American experience at a fraction of the scale. Greenglow published its interest, attracted a brief round of press attention — British newspapers enjoyed a national antigravity programme as much as American ones had in 1955 — and then, finding no effect that survived scrutiny and no theoretical opening its consultants could endorse, the company quietly shelved the file. No scandal attended the shelving, because no promise had been made that shelving could betray.

Greenglow's value is as a control experiment. A second firm, in a second country, ran the same inquiry a generation later and reached the same resting point: the physics permits study, the claims do not yet permit investment. That is not proof of impossibility. It is the shape honest negative evidence takes.

20The Internet Era

A lifter rising on a forum video thumbnail — the most-reproduced antigravity demonstration in history, mid-replication

The internet made replication nearly free and legend nearly immortal, at the same time, in the same feed. From the late 1990s onward, plans, photographs, and video let any builder with a high-voltage supply reproduce the classic demonstrations within days. Claims that once took a decade to travel by newsletter now circled the world before breakfast.

The emblematic device of the era is the lifter: a featherweight frame of balsa, foil, and fine wire, descended directly from Townsend Brown's capacitors and popularised online above all by French experimenter Jean-Louis Naudin. Under thirty thousand volts or so, a lifter rises briskly on its tether and hovers, humming, in a way that video presents as pure antigravity. Thousands were built; for a generation of enthusiasts, the lifter was the antigravity demonstration.

It is explained, and Hall II has already done so: the lifter flies on ion wind, in air, and not in vacuum, as careful tests confirmed for a widening audience. Yet the era's asymmetry persisted — the rising lifter was vivid, short, and shareable, while its vacuum failure was a paragraph in a test report. Replication had sped up for devices and slowed down, relatively, for verdicts, because a verdict cannot be filmed rising off a bench.

That asymmetry is the environment the rest of this page must work in. Hall III turns from institutions to physics — the genuine openings modern supporters actually cite — and Hall IV to the laboratory claims of the last thirty years. Each will arrive trailing video. The charter of Block 3 and the balance of Block 10 travel with them. In the internet era, the labels are not decoration; they are the only load-bearing structure a reader gets.

21What the History Teaches

Read in sequence, the century from Cavorite to the internet lifter repeats with unusual regularity. A period of enthusiasm opens the subject to money and attention. Inside that opening someone reports a real anomaly, or mistakes a familiar effect for a new one. The report travels faster than the measurement that would settle it.

The missing step is nearly always the same. A control experiment is postponed: the apparatus is not run reversed, not run in vacuum, not run with the suspected cause removed. Without that control, an ion wind can look like gravity control, a cable force can look like thrust, and a balance disturbed by heat or vibration can appear to record weight loss. The anomaly does not need to be invented to be wrong.

Then the ending arrives quietly. Funding moves to work that delivers, journals lose interest, and the file closes without a retraction dramatic enough to correct the legend. No villain is required to explain this pattern. Enthusiasm, confusion, and an omitted control are sufficient, and institutions that tire of a question are not the same as institutions that have answered it falsely.

That is why this history matters to a supporter. The opponent is not a person guarding a secret. It is a repeatable failure of method, and a failure of method can be corrected. The next claim, whatever it is, deserves the control experiment the last one never received.

22A Note on Suppression Stories

A closed archive box beside an open laboratory notebook, the record that exists weighed against the record that is only imagined.

The buried-invention story deserves a respectful hearing because it answers a real question: if a working device existed, where did it go? In its usual form, an inventor succeeds, officials recognise the consequences, and the machine, the papers, and sometimes the people disappear into secrecy. The story is coherent. Coherence, however, is not evidence.

Evidence for suppression would have a recognisable shape. There would be dated laboratory records, procurement and patent trails, independent witnesses describing the same apparatus, or a later test reproducing the reported effect under controlled conditions. A classified program on this scale would also leave a perimeter: budgets, facilities, personnel, and the scientific consequences of work built upon it. After a century, some part of that perimeter should be visible.

The century we can document points the other way. Governments have proved unable to keep far smaller secrets indefinitely. Wartime radar, jet propulsion, nuclear weapons, reconnaissance satellites, and stealth aircraft were all classified, and all entered the public record through leaks, declassification, or visible use. Even the Manhattan Project, protected by wartime secrecy and compartmentalisation, was penetrated and then extensively documented.

Absence of evidence is not absolute proof that nothing was ever hidden. It is, however, a strong argument against a hidden, working antigravity technology maintained for generations without a reproducible trace. A supporter does the cause no favour by preferring the legend to the archive. If a device worked, the record should eventually show it working.

23First, Credit Where Due

A small magnet floating steadily above a dark superconducting disc, vapour curling from liquid nitrogen around its base.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Levitation is not a rumour. Objects are held against gravity every day in university laboratories, hospitals, factories, railway systems, and the International Space Station. The effects are filmed, measured, sold as instruments, and taught to undergraduates. This hall records them before the page turns to claims that have not met the same standard.

That order is deliberate. A page sceptical of antigravity could be mistaken for a page sceptical of wonder, and the two positions are different. A frog can float in a magnetic field. A train can carry passengers without touching its guideway. A droplet can hang in a node of sound while a camera records its shape. None of this needs exaggeration.

Each method in this hall also has a boundary stated beside it. Levitation supports a weight by applying an opposing force through a field, a surface, sound, charge, or light. It does not make the supported object weigh less. The Earth pulls on the frog, the train, and the droplet with undiminished force throughout the demonstration.

This is the page's deposit of good faith. Where the evidence is established, it says so without qualification. That same plainness will govern the later halls, where the evidence is weaker. Credit given here is what makes refusal elsewhere a judgement rather than a prejudice.

24The Levitating Frog

A living frog suspended, legs splayed, inside the cylindrical bore of a high-field magnet at Nijmegen.

Status · ESTABLISHED

In 1997, at the High Field Magnet Laboratory in Nijmegen, a living frog rose into the air inside a powerful electromagnet and remained there, apparently unharmed. The experiment was performed by Andre Geim and colleagues, building on work with Michael Berry. A strawberry, a grasshopper, and water itself could be floated in the same apparatus.

The field reached about 16 tesla, roughly 300,000 times stronger than the Earth's magnetic field at the surface. That figure can mislead if read alone. What levitates a diamagnetic body is not field strength by itself but the product of the field and its gradient. In the shaped field inside the magnet, that upward magnetic force on the water in the frog's body could balance the downward force of gravity.

Water is weakly diamagnetic: its molecules are very slightly repelled by a magnetic field. Most living tissue is mostly water, so the force acts throughout the body rather than through a harness at one point. The frog was not pulled up by its skin or supported from below. Every part of it was, in effect, falling upward at the same rate gravity pulled it down.

Berry and Geim received the Ig Nobel Prize in Physics in 2000, an award for work that first makes people laugh and then makes them think. Geim shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2010 for work on graphene. The sequence is sometimes told as a joke redeemed. It is better read as the same habit at work: take a neglected effect seriously enough to calculate and demonstrate it.

25Diamagnetism: The Honest Arithmetic

Status · ESTABLISHED

All matter is diamagnetic to some degree, though in iron and other ferromagnetic materials the effect is buried under a far stronger attraction. Water has a volume magnetic susceptibility of about minus nine parts per million. The minus sign means repulsion from a magnet. The parts per million explain why the repulsion is ordinarily invisible.

For water, stable levitation requires the product of magnetic field and field gradient to reach about 1,400 tesla squared per metre. The Nijmegen frog met that condition in a bore only a few centimetres across, at about 16 tesla. A human body has nearly the same susceptibility per kilogram as the frog, so in principle the local force requirement does not rise with size. The engineering requirement rises brutally.

The field must fill a human-sized volume, remain stable, and change at the right rate across that volume. The strongest continuous magnetic field achieved in a laboratory is about 45 tesla, in a hybrid magnet at the National High Magnetic Field Laboratory in Florida, with a bore measured in millimetres. Large resistive magnets consume tens of megawatts and require immense cooling. A magnet wide enough for a person, at frog-levitation conditions, lies far beyond present construction.

This is the honest arithmetic. Diamagnetic levitation is not forbidden by physics; it is rationed by magnet engineering, power, materials stress, and cost. It also remains levitation, not antigravity. The field pushes on the body from outside. Remove the magnet, and the full weight returns at once, because it never left.

26Superconducting Levitation

A permanent magnet hovering above a superconductor cooled below its transition temperature, field lines bending around the cold disc.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Cool certain materials below a critical temperature and their electrical resistance falls to zero. They also become strongly diamagnetic. A superconductor placed in a magnetic field generates persistent surface currents that cancel the field inside it, expelling the field from its interior. Walther Meissner and Robert Ochsenfeld described this effect in 1933.

Place a small permanent magnet above a cooled superconductor and the expelled field can support it. The magnet settles at a height where magnetic repulsion balances its weight. Unlike the unstable arrangements of ordinary magnets, this configuration can be stable without continuous adjustment, because any movement changes the induced currents in a way that opposes the movement.

High-temperature superconductors made the demonstration portable. Yttrium barium copper oxide becomes superconducting at about 92 kelvin, above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen at 77 kelvin. A disc cooled in liquid nitrogen can therefore levitate a magnet on a laboratory bench, in a classroom, or in front of a camera, for as long as it stays cold.

No power is spent hovering once the magnet is in place, though power or cryogen is spent keeping the superconductor cold. The distinction matters. The magnet's weight has not changed, and the superconductor and its support carry the load. What superconductivity offers is a stable, frictionless magnetic support of remarkable elegance, not an exemption from gravity.

27Flux Pinning: The Quantum Lock

A magnet locked beneath a superconducting track, holding its distance and angle as the track is tilted.

Status · ESTABLISHED

The familiar video begins with a magnet that will not fall. It hangs below a superconducting track, follows it around a curve, and keeps its orientation when the track is inverted. The effect looks like a new kind of attachment because, in a precise sense, it is one: flux pinning.

A type-II superconductor does not expel a strong magnetic field completely. Above a threshold, the field enters in discrete, thread-like regions called vortices, each carrying a quantum of magnetic flux. Crystal defects and impurities can pin those vortices in place. Once the superconductor is cooled in the presence of a magnet, the pattern of field through it is effectively remembered and resisted.

That memory produces the quantum lock. Move the magnet away and the pinned field pulls it back. Move it closer and repulsion increases. Twist it and the trapped vortices resist the change. The magnet is therefore constrained in position and orientation in three dimensions, with a small gap maintained between the surfaces. It can ride above a track or hang beneath it with the same stability.

No power is consumed by the lock itself, and no gravity has been shielded. The force is transmitted through the superconductor to whatever holds it. Flux pinning is real quantum behaviour made visible at bench scale, which is why the videos endure. It needs no embellishment, and it promises vehicles only where a prepared superconducting track can be built and kept cold.

28Maglev: The Train That Floats

A white SCMaglev train lifted clear of its U-shaped guideway on the Yamanashi test line in Japan.

Status · ESTABLISHED

On 21 April 2015, a seven-car SCMaglev train on Central Japan Railway Company's Yamanashi test track reached 603 kilometres per hour, the world speed record for a crewed railway vehicle. Superconducting magnets aboard the train interact with coils in the guideway to lift it about ten centimetres, guide it laterally, and propel it without wheel contact at speed.

A different system has carried the public for two decades. Shanghai's Transrapid, built with German technology, opened commercially in 2004 and links the city with Pudong International Airport over about 30 kilometres. Its vehicles use conventional electromagnets attracted upward toward the guideway, held at a gap of roughly a centimetre by rapid control. At lower speeds the same family of ideas serves airport and urban lines in Japan, South Korea, and China.

The engineering gains are real: no rolling friction from load-bearing wheels, less mechanical wear, and smooth acceleration. The costs are equally real. A maglev vehicle cannot leave its guideway, switch routes as freely as a steel-wheel train, or use existing track. The guideway is not scenery. It is the active partner that supplies lift, guidance, and thrust along every metre of the journey.

Maglev therefore marks both the summit of practical levitation and its clearest limit. A train weighing hundreds of tonnes can float at aircraft speed, provided a purpose-built structure floats it. It pushes on that structure, not on gravity. The planet's pull on every passenger remains exactly what it was at the station.

29Magnetic Bearings and Flywheels

Status · ESTABLISHED

Most working levitation is never seen. In magnetic bearings, electromagnets hold a rotating shaft clear of any solid surface while sensors measure its position many times per second and controllers adjust the currents to keep it centred. Turbines, compressors, pumps, and machine tools use the arrangement where friction, wear, lubrication, or contamination would limit a mechanical bearing.

The same support makes a flywheel into a battery. A heavy rotor is spun to tens of thousands of revolutions per minute inside a vacuum enclosure, often on magnetic bearings that remove most contact loss. Electrical energy becomes kinetic energy; when power is needed, the rotor drives a generator and slows. Commercial units smooth fluctuations in electrical grids, protect data centres during interruptions, and recover energy in industrial systems.

These applications are unglamorous because they solve bounded problems. The shaft still has weight, and the bearing housing carries it. The flywheel still obeys conservation of energy. Levitation removes friction at the interface, which can be the difference between a machine that lasts and one that consumes itself, but it creates no energy and cancels no mass.

That quiet economy is a useful correction to the spectacle elsewhere in this hall. Engineers buy levitation when it reduces wear, noise, contamination, or loss by a measurable amount. They do not buy it as a symbol. The technology earned its place by surviving cost accounts and maintenance schedules, standards any future gravity technology will also have to meet.

30Acoustic Levitation

A row of small droplets held motionless at the nodes of a standing sound wave between two ultrasonic transducers.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Sound pushes. A sufficiently intense sound wave exerts radiation pressure on anything that reflects or absorbs it, and when two waves are arranged to form a standing wave, that push organises itself into layers. At the pressure nodes, small objects can be trapped and held against gravity in mid-air.

Laboratory acoustic levitators commonly use ultrasound, often around 40 kilohertz, at sound pressure levels far above anything a room should contain for listening. They hold water droplets, solvents, small electronic parts, and insects for study, typically at masses from milligrams to a few grams. Arrays of transducers can move a trapped particle along a path, rotate it, or bring two droplets together to start a reaction.

The scientific value is not the floating itself. A droplet held by sound touches no container wall, so it cannot be contaminated by a crucible or seeded into crystallising by a surface. Chemists can study supercooled liquids, evaporation, mixing, and reactions in isolation. Pharmaceutical researchers use the method to form amorphous solids that would be difficult to prepare in contact with a vessel.

Scale is the boundary. Acoustic force must compete with weight, air currents, heating, and the violence of the sound field itself. What works elegantly for a droplet does not scale straightforwardly to a tool, still less to a person. It is nevertheless complete levitation, achieved with loudspeakers and mathematics: the air is pushed, and the air pushes back.

31Electrostatic Levitation

A glowing molten sample suspended between electrodes inside a space-station levitation furnace, touching nothing at all.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Give a small sample an electric charge, place it between electrodes, and adjust the voltages quickly enough, and electrostatic force can hold it in empty space. Position sensors watch the sample, a control system corrects its drift thousands of times in effect, and lasers or induction heaters can then melt it while it touches nothing.

NASA developed electrostatic levitation for materials research, and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency operates an Electrostatic Levitation Furnace aboard the International Space Station. Samples there have included refractory metals, ceramics, and alloys heated to temperatures above 2,000 degrees Celsius. In orbit, gravity is so effectively reduced that far weaker fields can position a sample than would be needed to lift it on Earth.

Containerless processing is the point. A molten reactive metal in a crucible reacts with the crucible. A liquid cooled below its freezing point in contact with a wall tends to crystallise at that contact. Suspended in vacuum or inert gas, the same material can be supercooled, its density and viscosity measured, and its solidification watched without the container inventing the result.

Electrostatic levitation is therefore one of the finest laboratory instruments in this hall. It has produced reliable data on materials later used in turbines, electronics, and spacecraft. It has not reduced the weight of anything. On the ground it can barely lift its gram-scale samples; in space it is a pair of invisible hands, not a repeal of gravity.

32Optical Levitation

A focused laser beam trapping a single microscopic particle in darkness, its scattered light marking the point of capture.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Light carries momentum. When a laser beam is focused tightly, the intensity gradient can draw a transparent microscopic particle toward the brightest region, while radiation pressure along the beam helps hold it there. Arthur Ashkin developed this single-beam trap, known as optical tweezers, at Bell Laboratories in the 1980s, after earlier work levitating particles with laser light.

The forces are measured in piconewtons, trillionths of a newton, and the objects are bacteria, viruses, organelles, strands of DNA, and individual molecules attached to microscopic beads. That smallness is not a defect. Optical tweezers let biologists pull on a molecular motor, measure the strength of a chemical bond, or sort cells without touching them mechanically.

Ashkin shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2018 for the optical tweezers and their application to biological systems. By then, related optical traps had moved into fundamental physics. Silica nanoparticles about a hundred nanometres across can be levitated in vacuum, isolated from nearly every disturbance, and cooled toward their quantum ground state of motion.

Such a particle becomes an extraordinary force sensor. Laboratories use optically levitated systems to search for tiny impulses, test the limits of quantum mechanics at increasing mass scales, and develop sensors for acceleration and dark-matter interactions. The masses remain almost inconceivably small. What has grown is not the load but the precision with which the load can be heard.

33Radiation Pressure and the Solar Sail

A vast, gossamer solar sail turning in sunlight above the Earth, propelled by photons alone.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Sunlight arriving at Earth carries pressure as well as warmth. On a perfectly reflecting surface at our distance from the Sun, that pressure is about nine micronewtons per square metre: the weight of a grain of sand spread over a sail the size of a room. It is almost nothing. In space, given months and years, almost nothing accumulates into a change of orbit.

Japan's IKAROS spacecraft proved the principle beyond theoretical doubt. Launched by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency in 2010, it deployed a sail about 14 metres across, accelerated by radiation pressure, generated power with thin-film solar cells embedded in the membrane, and steered in part with liquid-crystal devices that changed reflectivity at the sail's edge. Its thrust was roughly a millinewton, and it flew by Venus.

The Planetary Society's LightSail 2, launched in 2019, made the demonstration in Earth orbit. Its 32-square-metre sail raised the spacecraft's apogee measurably over successive orbits, using sunlight alone and no propellant. Mission controllers oriented the sail toward and away from the Sun on each pass, turning a force too weak to feel into a navigational instrument.

A solar sail is the only craft in this hall that travels rather than hovers, and the qualification matters. It does not levitate against gravity; the Sun's photons push it while gravity continues to shape its orbit. Its lesson is patience as a propulsion strategy. Given a large, light structure and no deadline measured in days, light itself is enough.

34Why None of This Is Antigravity

Four diagrams in a row, a magnet, a sound wave, a charged sample, and a sail, each arrow of support traced back to something outside the floating object.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Every method in this hall can be audited by following the force. The frog is pushed by a magnetic field produced in a magnet bolted to the Earth. The maglev train pushes down and along its guideway. The acoustic droplet pushes on air. The electrostatic sample pushes on electrodes. The solar sail exchanges momentum with sunlight arriving from outside the spacecraft.

In each case the supported object's weight is unchanged. Put it on a scale in the only way the physics allows, and gravity's claim is intact. What the apparatus supplies is an equal opposing force, paid for in electrical power, cryogen, sound energy, structural strength, or time. Levitation is a transaction, with the accounts open for inspection.

Antigravity, as this page defines it, would close those accounts differently. A mass inside a shielded region would itself weigh less. A balance would read lower in vacuum, with fields excluded and no support force to trace. No experiment in this hall reports that result, and none claims to. Their authors describe levitation accurately, which is why their work can be trusted.

The distinction is not a disappointment imposed after the fact. It is what makes the hall useful to the program. These are working examples of forces that oppose gravity, measured to their limits. A genuine reduction of weight would have to look different from all of them, and now the reader has the comparison set by which to recognise it.

35What Levitation Teaches the Program

Status · ESTABLISHED

The levitation literature is full of numbers a stranger can check. A paper states the field, the gradient, the sample mass, the temperature, the stability, and the uncertainty. Another laboratory can build the apparatus from the description, and manufacturers can sell a version of it. The result does not depend on being present on a fortunate afternoon.

Controls are built into the demonstration rather than added as an apology. Turn off the field and the frog's support vanishes. Warm the superconductor through its transition and the magnet falls. Move the acoustic sample away from a node and the trap lets go. Cause and effect can be separated because the apparatus lets an experimenter change one variable at a time and watch the result change with it.

Calibration is the third habit. Balances are tested with known masses, fields are mapped with probes, and forces are compared against calculations that were written down before the applause. When a measured value departs from theory, the departure is reported in the same units as the agreement. Error bars are not a confession of weakness. They are the coordinates of the result.

This is the apprenticeship the program asks of its supporters. Learn how a real effect behaves under hostile questioning before judging an extraordinary one. The propellantless files in a later hall fail, where they fail, at exactly these habits: the missing control, the unpublished calibration, the number without its uncertainty. Levitation teaches what a passing claim would have to do.

36The Home Laboratory

A thin sheet of pyrolytic graphite floating above a checkerboard of small neodymium magnets on a careful experimenter's bench.

Status · ESTABLISHED

A home experimenter can begin with the strongest diamagnetic material readily available. A thin sheet of pyrolytic graphite will float above an array of neodymium magnets arranged with alternating poles. The gap is small, the load is a fraction of a gram, and the effect is genuine diamagnetic levitation, visible without a university magnet laboratory.

Commercial kits extend the bench. A magnet can be suspended beneath an electromagnet whose current is adjusted by a position sensor, or spun in the field of a base unit. Each kit is also a lesson in the hall's limits: the stable arrangements are specific, the supported masses are modest, and the instructions matter less than measuring what the device actually holds, for how long, and at what power.

Safety belongs in the procedure, not in a footnote. Strong neodymium magnets can pinch skin hard enough to injure, shatter when they collide, damage magnetic media, and must be kept away from pacemakers and other medical implants. Small magnets are a serious swallowing hazard for children. Eye protection is appropriate when magnets may snap together or chip.

The lifter described in the next hall adds high voltage to the bench, and with it a stricter rule: current-limited supplies, insulated leads, discharge after every run, no work alone, and no mains wiring improvised. A careful home laboratory is defined by these habits. It records mass, voltage, distance, and weather, repeats the run, and writes down the failures in the same notebook as the flights.

37Levitation's Frontier

An engineer's rendering of a quantum-locked vehicle above a prepared track, the route drawn in and the open country left deliberately blank.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Flux pinning invites a recurring proposal: vehicles locked above a superconducting track, stable at any angle, moving with almost no friction. The physics of a demonstration model is sound. The open questions are the track's cost, the refrigeration along its length, the behaviour at switches and in emergencies, and whether any route carries enough traffic to repay a guideway unlike every road and railway already built.

Levitation has also been studied as a way to help launch spacecraft. In the 1990s and 2000s, NASA and others examined maglev launch assist, in which a rocket or test vehicle would be accelerated along a track before its engines took over. Studies and subscale tracks showed that the acceleration was feasible. Saving the first portion of a launch does not remove the rocket equation from the remainder, and no operational system followed.

When engineers spend levitation money today, it goes mainly to transport systems already ordered, semiconductor and precision machinery, energy storage, bearings, vibration isolation, and research instruments. Quantum levitation sensors and larger superconducting magnets attract funding because they extend tools with known uses. The frontier is therefore not a single breakthrough waiting for belief. It is a set of cost curves waiting to cross.

A supporter should find that encouraging rather than deflating. Levitation began as a laboratory curiosity and became infrastructure by solving specific problems at defensible prices. If a gravity technology ever becomes practical, its first form will probably look similar: narrow, expensive, indispensable in one place, and easy to mistake for a disappointment from a distance.

38Hall Verdict: The Floor of Fact

Status · ESTABLISHED

The hall can be read as a table in prose. Diamagnetic levitation uses magnetic fields up to tens of tesla to support grams to kilograms in small volumes, and is a mature laboratory method. Superconducting levitation and flux pinning support magnets and model vehicles with no hovering power, provided the superconductor stays cold. Maglev scales the principle to trains of hundreds of tonnes, at the price of a dedicated guideway.

The smaller methods are no less established. Acoustic levitation holds milligrams to grams for containerless chemistry. Electrostatic levitation positions molten samples for precision measurement, especially in orbit. Optical traps hold piconewton-scale biological structures and nanoparticles that now serve as frontier instruments. Radiation pressure moves spacecraft of sail area in tens of square metres with thrust measured in millinewtons over months.

Readiness follows use, not spectacle. Magnetic bearings, flywheels, maglev lines, levitation furnaces, and optical tweezers are commercial or operational. Human diamagnetic levitation is physically allowed and engineering-infeasible. Quantum-locked transport is demonstrated at model scale and unbuilt as infrastructure. Each status can be dated, priced, and revised without moving any other row.

This is the floor of fact on which the rest of the page stands. Everything in this hall works, and none of it is antigravity. Both halves of that sentence must survive together. A reader who keeps them together is ready for the next hall, where devices unquestionably fly and the question is only what, exactly, is pushing them.

39The Lifter on the Bench

A triangular lifter of balsa, wire, and foil rising from a bench, its thin power lead trailing to a high-voltage supply.

Status · ESTABLISHED

The lifter is disarmingly simple. A frame of balsa wood or drinking straws carries a fine wire around its top edge and a strip of aluminium foil around its base. Connect the wire to one terminal of a high-voltage supply and the foil to the other, apply typically 20,000 to 40,000 volts at very low current, and a well-built triangle a few tens of centimetres across rises from the bench.

It has been built in school laboratories, workshops, and kitchens in many countries, and filmed so often that it became the most reproduced antigravity demonstration of the internet era. The craft is real. Builders learn to keep the frame light, the wire smooth, the foil edge even, and the tether free, because small asymmetries decide whether the device climbs, tilts, or merely strains against its leads.

Respect for those hands is appropriate here. A lifter is not a cardboard model of a claim; it is a working electrical aircraft with no propeller, no jet, and no visible moving part. It rises in silence apart from the hiss of corona discharge, sometimes carrying a payload of a few grams, and it invites exactly the question a good experiment should invite: what is providing the force?

The answer has been tested as carefully as the device deserves. It lies in the air around the wires, and it sets the boundary of this entire hall. The lifter flies. It does not fly everywhere, and the place where it stops flying is the most informative result in its long history.

40What Actually Pushes It

Arrows of ionised air streaming downward from a lifter's wire to its foil skirt, the invisible propellant made visible in diagram.

Status · ESTABLISHED

The lifter is an electrohydrodynamic device. At the fine wire, the electric field is intense enough to ionise nearby air molecules, stripping electrons and leaving positive ions. Those ions are repelled by the wire and attracted toward the foil electrode. On the way they collide with neutral air molecules, transferring momentum downward. The reaction to that moving air is an upward force on the craft.

The propellant, in other words, is the atmosphere itself. The lifter is a fan without blades: electricity organises air into a jet, and the jet carries the frame. Its efficiency is poor, its thrust falls as air thins, and its performance depends on humidity, electrode geometry, and pressure in ways that a gravitational effect would have no reason to respect.

Vacuum chambers supply the decisive control. As pressure is reduced, lifter thrust falls with the supply of ionisable air. In experiments conducted from the early 2000s onward, including tests associated with NASA researchers and independent laboratories, no thrust remained once corona and ion-wind effects were removed at high vacuum. A device coupled to gravity should not care whether the chamber has been pumped down. This one does, completely.

So the claim dies and the device still flies, and both facts belong in the record. Electrohydrodynamic thrust has since carried a fixed-wing aircraft in work described in the next part of this hall. The lifter was never evidence against gravity. It is evidence that air, properly electrified, can be made to push, and that a vacuum chamber is the honest courtroom for telling the difference.

41The MIT Ion Plane

A fixed-wing aircraft with no propeller or turbine, its wings lined with thin wires and foil electrodes, in steady indoor flight.

Status · ESTABLISHED

In November 2018, a team led by Steven Barrett at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology reported in Nature the first sustained flight of a heavier-than-air aeroplane powered by electrohydrodynamic thrust. The aircraft had a wingspan of five metres, weighed 2.45 kilograms, and had no propeller, turbine, or other moving propulsion part. In tests inside MIT's duPont Athletic Center it flew about 60 metres, the length available, in roughly ten seconds, and repeated the flight ten times.

The propulsion is the lifter principle made orderly. Wires held at about +20,000 volts and foil collectors at about -20,000 volts ionise nitrogen molecules in the air between them. The ions collide with neutral air as they accelerate toward the collectors, producing an ionic wind. The aircraft is pushed opposite to that wind. Batteries and a high-voltage converter were carried on board, so the flight was self-powered, not tethered.

Barrett's group stated the limits in the same paper. Ionic wind is inefficient compared with propellers and jets, needs very light structures, and works only where there is air to ionise. Thrust per unit area remains small, and scaling to a passenger aircraft is not in prospect.

This is therefore both a vindication and a ceiling. The electrostatic effect that lifter builders demonstrated on benches can propel a real aeroplane, silently, in air. It propels nothing in vacuum, and it reduces the aircraft's weight not at all.

42Townsend Brown, Revisited

Status · ESTABLISHED

Thomas Townsend Brown (1905-1985) was an American experimenter who, working in the 1920s with physicist Paul Alfred Biefeld at Denison University in Ohio, reported that a highly charged capacitor tended to move toward its positive pole. Brown pursued the observation for decades, filed a series of patents on electrostatic propulsion devices from the 1920s into the 1960s, and named the phenomenon the Biefeld-Brown effect.

What Brown observed in air was genuine. A capacitor or pointed electrode at tens of thousands of volts ionises the surrounding air, and the resulting ion flow produces a measurable force. Later investigators reproduced that force, explained it as electrohydrodynamic thrust, and measured how it falls as air pressure falls. In a sufficiently good vacuum, for the asymmetric capacitors Brown described, the thrust falls to zero within measurement limits.

Brown himself interpreted the effect more largely, as an interaction between electricity and gravitation, and used the word electrogravitics. The name outlived the evidence. Trade-press enthusiasm in the 1950s, classified-research speculation, and later retellings converted a real atmospheric effect into a claimed gravity control that Brown never publicly demonstrated under controlled conditions.

The biography matters because the mislabeling happened once, in identifiable steps: observation, patent, interpretation, legend. Each step added a claim the previous step had not established. The registry keeps Brown in the record, with credit for the observation and without credit for the conclusion it could not support.

43Coulombcraft and the Vacuum Test

Status · ESTABLISHED

Coulombcraft is a useful collective name for the family built on electrostatic forces: lifters, asymmetric capacitors, and ionocraft of every bench size. Their common feature is not a shape but a dependence. The force they produce is mediated by charged particles, and near Earth those particles are supplied by the air the device ionises.

The scaling follows from that dependence, not from the size of the power supply alone. Raise the voltage and more air is ionised, up to corona and breakdown limits. Thin the air and there are fewer molecules to charge and to collide with, so thrust declines. Remove the air and the mechanism has no working fluid. Tests of lifter-type devices in vacuum chambers have repeatedly found no thrust beyond experimental error once electromagnetic and thermal artefacts are controlled.

This is why the vacuum chamber is the only honest courtroom for the whole family. In air, a lifter truly rises, and a photograph of it rising proves nothing about gravity. In vacuum, the rival explanations are separated: a gravitational effect, if there were one, should persist; ion wind cannot. The chamber is not a trick condition imposed by sceptics. It is the condition the claim itself requires.

The verdict is narrow and should be kept narrow. Coulombcraft are established as atmospheric electrostatic thrusters, sometimes elegant and sometimes useful for study. As evidence for modifying gravity, they have been tested in the relevant condition and have produced nothing.

44Beamed Energy: Light from the Ground

A small conical craft rising on a pulsed beam from below, the propellant and power plant left on the ground.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Beamed-energy propulsion separates the engine from the energy source. A laser or microwave installation on the ground, or in orbit, sends energy to a vehicle that carries little or no fuel for the climb. The vehicle may heat air or an onboard propellant with the beam, expand a plasma, or simply be pushed by the pressure of light itself.

The physics is established at small scale. In experiments led by Leik Myrabo of Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute in the 1990s and early 2000s, pulsed laser energy lifted small LightCraft test vehicles, reaching an altitude of about 71 metres in a 2000 flight at White Sands Missile Range. Microwave and laser thermal concepts have been studied by agencies and universities in the United States and Japan. Radiation pressure, the direct push of photons, is the same force demonstrated in space by the solar sails IKAROS and LightSail 2.

The obstacle is infrastructure, not principle. A useful launcher would need very large, precisely aimed transmitters, power in the gigawatt range for human-scale payloads, optics or antennas that hold focus over long distances, and safety systems for a beam that must never wander. Cost estimates therefore describe ground stations, not vehicles.

Nothing in the scheme reduces gravity. The craft is pushed, continuously and externally, for as long as the beam reaches it. It is recorded here because it solves a real part of the launch problem - leaving the propellant home - while making its dependence on a partner completely explicit.

45Lunar Dust, Levitating

Fine grey dust hovering just above the lunar surface at the terminator, lit low by the Sun where day meets night.

Status · ESTABLISHED

The Moon has almost no atmosphere, but its surface is electrically active. Ultraviolet sunlight knocks electrons from dust grains on the day side, leaving them positively charged; on the night side, grains collect electrons from the surrounding plasma and charge negatively. Near the terminator, where day meets night, the resulting electric fields can be strong enough to lift the finest charged grains a short distance above the regolith.

The observations are old and multiple. Robotic Surveyor landers in the 1960s photographed a horizon glow after sunset consistent with dust near the surface. Apollo 17 astronauts in 1972 sketched and reported streamers in the sky before orbital sunrise, interpreted as light scattered by dust above the surface. NASA's LADEE mission, which orbited the Moon in 2013-2014, measured an asymmetric cloud of dust maintained mainly by meteoroid impacts, a related but distinct population that engineers must also account for.

For engineers the effect is a nuisance with consequences. Levitated and mobilised dust coats optics and seals, abrades suits and mechanisms, and clings electrostatically to everything, as Apollo crews reported after each surface excursion. Future lunar installations must design for it in advance.

For this registry the datum is instructive. Here, on an airless body, electrostatic forces really do levitate material against gravity - measurably, predictably, and only for grains so fine that charge outweighs weight. Scale, as usual, is the whole story.

46Tethers: Trading Gravity for Rotation

A long tether turning end over end in orbit, a climber craft caught at one tip and released toward a higher trajectory.

Status · ESTABLISHED

A momentum-exchange tether does not defeat orbital mechanics; it trades within it. A long cable in orbit, set rotating end over end, can catch a payload at the low point of its swing and release it at the high point, throwing it into a higher orbit. The tether system loses exactly the momentum the payload gains, and must later regain it - from a returning payload, from propulsion, or from electrodynamic interaction with a planet's magnetic field.

The components have flown, in pieces. NASA and Italian Space Agency tethered satellite experiments on Space Shuttle missions in 1992 and 1996 deployed kilometres of tether and, on the second flight, generated electric current as the conducting cable crossed Earth's magnetic field, before the tether broke from an electrical fault. Small tether experiments since then have tested deployment, damping, and deorbiting. No rotating skyhook has yet carried a payload operationally.

Engineers including Robert P. Hoyt of Tethers Unlimited developed the engineering literature for rotating systems such as the rotovator, with named problems: materials strong and light enough, deployment dynamics, the timing of a rendezvous with a moving tether tip, and survival after micrometeoroid cuts. Each problem is stated in numbers and is, in principle, testable.

The result, if built, would look like flight without fuel and is therefore filed in this hall. Its honesty is in the ledger. Altitude is gained by bookkeeping with rotation and gravity together, and the books balance to the last joule.

47Balloon: The High Road

A vast scientific balloon at stratospheric altitude above the Earth's curved horizon, its instrument gondola hanging far below.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Buoyancy is the oldest working answer to gravity on this page. A volume of gas lighter than the air it displaces - heated air, hydrogen, or helium - experiences an upward force equal to the weight of the displaced air, as Archimedes described. No thrust is produced and no energy need be spent to remain aloft, beyond making good losses of gas, heat, or ballast.

The performance is modest in altitude and remarkable in economy. Large NASA scientific balloons lift instruments of several tonnes to altitudes around 37 to 42 kilometres, above most of the atmosphere, for flights lasting days, and long-duration balloons have circled Antarctica for weeks. Crewed stratospheric balloons reached 41.4 kilometres in Alan Eustace's 2014 free-fall flight. Airships trade some of that altitude for steerability, and designs for freight and surveillance airships return regularly to serious study because they combine endurance with very low energy use per hour aloft.

The limits are equally plain. Buoyant lift vanishes as the air thins, so a balloon cannot climb to orbit, or even most of the way there. Weather governs launch and recovery, volumes are enormous for the payload carried, and hydrogen's lifting advantage comes with flammability that helium avoids at higher cost and scarcity.

Buoyancy is therefore the cheapest antigravity substitute on Earth precisely because it makes no antigravity claim. It opposes weight with a displaced medium, openly, and stops where the medium stops.

48Hall Verdict: Air Is a Crutch

The hall in one view: an ion plane, a balloon, a tether and a beam of light, each tethered in its own way to something outside itself.

Status · ESTABLISHED

Every device in this hall flies, floats, rises, or is thrown - and every one of them needs something outside itself to do it. The ion plane needs air to ionise. Lunar dust needs sunlight, plasma, and grains fine enough for charge to matter. Beamed propulsion needs a station that stays behind and pushes. Tethers need the rotation and momentum of an orbital system. Balloons need an atmosphere to displace.

That dependence is not a defect to apologise for. It is the definition of the hall, and it is why the label on these blocks is ESTABLISHED. Each mechanism can be stated, calculated, and measured, and each fails in a predictable way when its medium or partner is removed. A vacuum chamber stills the lifter. Night and calm charge conditions settle the dust. Cut the beam and the beamed craft coasts. Thin the air and the balloon descends.

The dream that brought readers to the hall is different in kind: a force that needs neither medium nor partner, that acts on weight itself, in vacuum, on demand. Nothing exhibited here is that force, and several exhibits are valuable precisely because they show how completely the familiar substitutes account for the familiar demonstrations.

The hall therefore closes with credit given and the boundary drawn. Flight without wings is a real and growing engineering art. Antigravity, in the strict sense this page uses, remains unexhibited - and the next hall contains the claims that said otherwise.

49The Shape of the Claim

A propellantless drive, as the term is used in this hall, is a closed box claimed to accelerate its own centre of mass in empty space while ejecting nothing, pushing on nothing, and exchanging no momentum with its surroundings. The box may contain motors, microwaves, gyroscopes, or oscillating masses. From outside, the claim is always the same shape.

That shape collides with the conservation of momentum, one of the most thoroughly tested regularities in physics. In every verified interaction, the total momentum of an isolated system is conserved; a rocket moves because exhaust carries momentum the other way, and a sail moves because arriving and departing light carries it. A closed box that gained momentum from nothing would not merely be a better engine. It would require that regularity to fail, selectively, inside the box.

Internal motion alone cannot do it. Parts inside a closed system can move, spin, heat, and vibrate, and the centre of mass of the whole will stay where physics places it unless an external force acts. This is why so many devices in this hall move convincingly on a bench and not at all on a proper balance: the bench supplies the external interaction the claim had overlooked.

The burden of proof is therefore total, and it is not personal. It does not doubt an inventor's sincerity or skill. It states, in advance, the size of what must be overturned, so that everyone - claimant and tester alike - knows what a convincing experiment would have to show.

50The Dean Drive

A 1950s bench mechanism of spinning, eccentric weights in a brass frame, of the kind promoted as a reactionless drive.

Status · DISPROVEN

The Dean drive, promoted in the 1950s by inventor Norman L. Dean, used rotating eccentric masses and a ratcheting mechanism intended to convert circular motion into a one-way push. Dean obtained a United States patent in 1959 for a system for converting rotary motion into unidirectional motion, and became famous through science-fiction editor John W. Campbell, who championed it in Astounding Science Fiction in 1960.

Demonstrations of the period typically showed the apparatus creeping, lifting one side of a scale, or producing an apparent change in weight while its parts spun. Such behaviour is real and explicable. An oscillating or rotating machine on a support exerts varying forces on that support; friction is not symmetric through a vibration cycle, and a bathroom scale is not an instrument for measuring net thrust from a vibrating load.

When devices of this class are mounted so that steady force can be separated from vibration - on a sensitive balance, suspended, or allowed to operate over complete cycles - the claimed net thrust does not survive. The centre of mass follows the ordinary rules, and apparent gains in one part of a cycle are repaid in another, or are spent against friction with the floor, the air, or the measuring apparatus.

The Dean drive is the template case for this hall not because it was the least sincere, but because it was the clearest. Magazine fame, a patent, and vivid demonstrations were all present. A proper balance was the only element missing, and when it was supplied, the thrust was gone.

51The EM Drive Arrives

A tapered copper resonant cavity, closed at both ends, of the form claimed to produce thrust from microwaves alone.

Status · UNCONFIRMED

In 2001 British engineer Roger Shawyer, through his company Satellite Propulsion Research, began promoting a resonant cavity thruster that became known as the EM drive. The device is a closed, tapered metal cavity - in effect a can wider at one end than the other - into which microwaves are fed. Shawyer claimed that radiation pressure, differing between the ends because of the taper, produced a net thrust with no propellant expelled.

The claim was explicit about what did not leave the device. There was no exhaust, no beam, and no claimed interaction with an external field sufficient to account for the reported force. If sustained thrust were real at the levels claimed, momentum would be created inside a closed system, and the drive could in principle accelerate indefinitely from an onboard power source alone.

The theory offered alongside the hardware drew objections on those grounds from the start. Radiation pressure inside a closed cavity acts on all its walls, and a full accounting, including the fields and the cavity together, gives no net external force. Critics also noted that claimed performance figures changed between presentations and were not, at arrival, supported by a peer-reviewed experimental paper that independent groups could replicate from a complete specification.

The label at arrival is therefore recorded as it stood: UNCONFIRMED. A claim had been made, a mechanism proposed and disputed, and no decisive independent test had yet been performed. What happened when such tests were performed occupies the next three blocks.

52Eagleworks

A copper cavity mounted on a sensitive torsion pendulum inside a vacuum chamber, the Eagleworks test configuration.

Status · UNCONFIRMED

Eagleworks was a small advanced-propulsion laboratory at NASA's Johnson Space Center, led by Harold G. White. In the mid-2010s the laboratory tested a tapered resonant cavity of the EM drive type on a torsion pendulum, including runs in vacuum intended to remove ion wind and much air-related error from the measurement.

The group's peer-reviewed paper, by White and colleagues in the Journal of Propulsion and Power in 2016, reported a force of about 1.2 millinewtons per kilowatt of input power, in both orientations of the cavity, with an uncertainty range that was a substantial fraction of the reported value. The authors presented the result as an anomalous thrust signal requiring further work, not as a working engine, and listed possible error sources that had not been fully excluded.

The paper's effect on the field was out of proportion to the size of the force. A NASA affiliation, a vacuum test, and publication in an established propulsion journal converted a fringe claim into a decade of argument. Subsequent discussion focused on forces the apparatus could not easily escape: heating and thermal expansion shifting the centre of mass, and electromagnetic forces between current-carrying cables and Earth's magnetic field acting on the pendulum.

Eagleworks is filed as UNCONFIRMED because that is what its own paper, read carefully, reported: micronewtons at the edge of the apparatus's ability to distinguish signal from artefact. The decisive question was whether a better instrument would resolve the signal upward - or resolve it away.

53The Dresden Verdict

A rebuilt cavity and balance in a Dresden laboratory, its power cables routed and shielded to hunt down a false thrust.

Status · DISPROVEN

The SpaceDrive project at Technische Universität Dresden, led by Martin Tajmar, set out to do what the EM drive debate most needed: rebuild the apparatus to a higher standard and account for every micronewton. The group constructed sensitive balances, tested cavity designs including a version based on the Eagleworks configuration, and published its methods and results openly, in papers and conference reports culminating in 2021.

The Dresden measurements found that apparent thrust tracked the experimental arrangements rather than the cavity's claimed principle. In particular, forces between the cables carrying power to the device and Earth's magnetic field could act on the balance and mimic the reported signal, and heating of the apparatus produced shifts that a sensitive pendulum reads as force. When cables were routed and shielded appropriately, when the device was suspended to reduce such couplings, and when control runs were compared, the thrust attributed to the cavity resolved to zero within the improved sensitivity.

The group applied the same discipline to other propellantless claims tested on its balances, with the same pattern: an initial signal, a mundane coupling identified, and no residual force once the coupling was removed. Tajmar summarised the cavity results publicly in 2021 as showing no thrust beyond the artefacts.

This is the most careful verdict the EM drive has received, from a laboratory that built the better instrument the earlier papers had called for. The registry records it accordingly: DISPROVEN as claimed, with the method preserved so any future claimant can be tested the same way.

54Where the EM Drive Stands

Status · DISPROVEN

The EM drive stands where the Dresden work left it. Every reported thrust that has been examined with an apparatus capable of tracing it has been resolved into a mundane cause: cable forces in a magnetic field, thermal expansion and centre-of-mass shift, air currents in tests not run in vacuum, or vibration and calibration error. No independent laboratory has reproduced a thrust that survives reversed controls, proper cable management, and thermal accounting at the same time.

That statement should be read with its scope attached. It does not prove that no cavity geometry, material, or power regime anywhere could ever show an anomaly. It records that the specific claim made since 2001 - usable thrust from a closed tapered cavity fed with microwaves - has been tested by the best available method and has not been found. In this registry, such a claim is labeled DISPROVEN as claimed.

Remaining defenders, recorded fairly, generally cite three things: the original Eagleworks numbers, which the Dresden analysis addressed but did not repeat run-for-run; tests reported outside peer review, often without complete cable, thermal, and control data; and the possibility that an unspecified variant differs from every cavity so far tested. Each citation is an argument for another fair test, not a result that displaces the existing one.

The door defined in Block 59 remains open to any such variant, under the same protocol. Until a claimant passes it, the shelf position of the EM drive does not change, and the page will not move it on the strength of a demonstration video.

55Woodward's Mach Effect

A stack of piezoelectric discs in a brass housing on a torsion balance, the Mach-effect thruster in its laboratory form.

Status · UNCONFIRMED

James F. Woodward, a physicist and historian at California State University, Fullerton, has developed since the 1990s a theory in which inertia itself is a gravitational interaction with the distant matter of the universe, following the line of thought Ernst Mach suggested and Dennis Sciama formalised in part. If inertia arises that way, Woodward argued in published papers, a mass whose energy fluctuates while accelerating should fluctuate in rest mass.

The experimental device built on that prediction, developed with colleagues including Heidi Fearn, uses a stack of piezoelectric crystals. Driven electrically, the stack is made to expand and contract while its internal energy varies in a timed pattern intended to exploit the predicted mass fluctuation and produce a steady force. Successive versions are known as Mach-effect thrusters, and a larger configuration as the MEGA drive.

The work has received institutional support of the legitimate kind described in Block 60. Woodward's team was selected for a NASA Innovative Advanced Concepts Phase I award in 2017 and a Phase II award that followed, funding study and testing of the concept. Published results have reported small forces in the micronewton range, obtained on sensitive balances, with the theory's defenders noting that the signals scale in the ways the model predicts.

Signals at the edge of noise, from one research line, over decades, define UNCONFIRMED in this registry. Woodward's case differs from the EM drive's in structure - the theory is public, quantitative, and prior to the device - and it is weighed on those terms in the next block.

56The Mach Effect, Weighed

Status · UNCONFIRMED

The Mach effect deserves a different gravestone from DISPROVEN, and this block explains why the registry has not carved it. The theory is published in peer-reviewed journals and in a book-length treatment, the predicted effect is stated in numbers before the apparatus runs, and the experimental programme has operated for decades in the open, inviting scrutiny. Those are the behaviours this page exists to reward, whatever the eventual verdict.

Against that stands the decisive absence. No laboratory independent of Woodward's group has replicated the reported thrust at a level that settles the question. The signals remain comparable in size to the known artefacts of sensitive balances - thermal drift, vibration, electromagnetic coupling - and critics, including participants in careful replication-oriented work, have argued that the devices are especially exposed to such effects because they are, by design, vibrating electromechanical stacks.

The theoretical foundation is also contested in the open literature. Whether Mach's principle, in the form the derivation requires, is a consequence of general relativity or an addition to it has been disputed, and the size of the predicted fluctuations depends on assumptions that other physicists do not share. A disputed theory with testable predictions is normal science; it is not yet a result.

The weighing therefore comes out level, which is what UNCONFIRMED means here. The claim could be resolved - by an independent replication on a Dresden-standard balance, published with raw data, in either direction. Until that experiment exists and is reported, moving the label in either direction would be a preference, not a measurement.

57Laithwaite's Gyroscopes

A heavy spinning gyroscope wheel on the end of a long pole, lifted one-handed in the demonstration that made the claim famous.

Status · DISPROVEN

Eric Laithwaite (1921-1997) was a professor of electrical engineering at Imperial College London, an inventor of the linear motor, and one of Britain's most gifted lecture demonstrators. In a lecture at the Royal Institution in London in 1974, he presented large gyroscopes and argued that their behaviour broke Newton's laws as usually taught. In the best-remembered demonstration, a spinning wheel of about 18 kilograms on a long shaft, which he could barely lift when still, could be raised overhead with one hand while it spun.

The demonstration was genuine theatre and genuine physics, but not the physics claimed. A spinning gyroscope resists reorientation and precesses: a torque applied to its axis produces motion at right angles to the expected direction. A demonstrator who works with the precession, guiding the shaft along the path the gyroscope is already taking, avoids the torques that make the stationary wheel so heavy to manoeuvre. The wheel's weight, measured on a scale, does not fall.

Torque analysis closes the case without impugning the performer. Through a complete motion, the forces and reactions between demonstrator, gyroscope, and ground account for the apparent ease, and no net upward force or loss of mass remains to be explained. Gyroscopes have since been tested directly on balances in many configurations, including claims modelled on Laithwaite's, without a reproducible weight change.

The Royal Institution episode is kept in this hall because fame and credentials were both present, and neither substituted for the balance. Laithwaite's real contributions to engineering stand elsewhere in the record, and are not diminished by this verdict.

58The Universal Artifact

A torsion balance in close view, surrounded by the quiet culprits of false thrust: warm cables, air currents and a humming mount.

Every bench in this hall, whatever it was testing, faced the same cast of impostors. This block is a field guide to them, because a reader who can name the artefacts can read any future thrust claim - including the primary papers - and know where to look first.

Heat is the most patient impostor. A device dissipating watts warms unevenly, expands, shifts its centre of mass on a balance arm, drives convection in any residual air, and can radiate or conduct asymmetrically. Vibration is the second: a humming or oscillating device interacts with its mount and with friction asymmetrically, creeping or registering force that vanishes when the full cycle and the mount are accounted for. Electromagnetic coupling is the third: currents in feed cables experience forces in Earth's magnetic field and in fields from the apparatus itself, and those forces travel straight down the cable to the balance.

To these add air currents and ion wind in tests not run in vacuum, seismic and building noise at the micronewton scale, outgassing from warming materials, and the human artefacts - runs selected, calibrations drifted, controls oriented the convenient way. None of these requires dishonesty. All of them produce micronewtons for free.

The universal artefact is therefore not a single error but a budget. A thrust claim is believable only when the paper presents that budget, line by line, with each impostor measured or bounded below the claimed signal. A paper without an artefact budget has not yet begun to answer its critics.

59How a Thrust Claim Should Die

This page's standard for propellantless claims is stated here in full, so that it can be applied to the next claim exactly as it was applied to the last. A claim that passes these conditions will be promoted on this page, prominently. A claim that fails them is not suppressed; it is filed, with the failed condition named.

The test must run in vacuum sufficient to exclude ion wind and convection, on a torsion balance or equivalent instrument whose sensitivity is demonstrated with calibrated forces before and after the runs. The device must be tested in reversed orientation, so that a real thrust reverses with the device while most artefacts do not, and with control runs - power applied to a dummy load, device inert, or cavity detuned - interleaved under the same conditions. Cables, heating, and magnetic interactions must be managed and reported, not assumed away.

The runs must include blinded or pre-registered elements where practicable: the analysis decided before the data are admired. Raw data, calibration records, and the full artefact budget must be published, so that a laboratory that did not build the device - the success criterion fixed in Block 9 of this page - can repeat the experiment from the paper alone.

Most claims here died condition by condition, as a better balance removed one impostor at a time and the signal shrank. That is not a cruel way for a claim to end. It is the only ending, or the only survival, that this registry will record.

60The NIAC Door

A modest meeting room door bearing a NASA program placard, open onto a corridor of laboratories - the audition room for impossible ideas.

The NASA Innovative Advanced Concepts program, known as NIAC, is the United States space agency's standing mechanism for examining ideas that are too early, and often too strange, for ordinary programme funding. Operating in its present form since 2011, with a predecessor institute from 1998 to 2007, NIAC makes phased study awards: small Phase I grants to define a concept, larger Phase II awards to develop the promising few, and rare Phase III work toward transition.

Propulsion claims have legitimately passed through this door. James Woodward's Mach-effect work, described in Blocks 55 and 56, received NIAC Phase I and Phase II support. So have concepts ranging from new sail architectures to fusion and antimatter studies and novel launch systems, each funded as a study - with analysis, modelling, and experiment - and not as an endorsement that the concept will work. Most NIAC concepts, in the program's own accounting, do not proceed to become missions.

That accounting is the point of the door. NIAC exists because an agency that funds only the probable will never be surprised, and an agency that funds claims without study will be surprised only by invoices. A phased award buys the right to find out, cheaply and in the open, whether a far-out idea survives its first quantitative examination.

For supporters, NIAC is the correct address for enthusiasm. An impossible idea with a mechanism, a prediction, and a test plan belongs in its audition room. An idea that refuses the audition has told the registry what it needs to know.

61What the Files Cost

The money in this hall was modest by the standards of aerospace. NASA's Breakthrough Propulsion Physics program, the most open official effort of its kind, spent about 1.2 million dollars between 1996 and 2002. Other sums followed individual claims — replication apparatus at Marshall Space Flight Center, test rigs at universities, years of salaries for careful experimenters — none of it itemised in one public ledger, and none of it large beside a single satellite.

Cost alone, however, is not the test of waste. A null result obtained with calibrated instruments, recorded controls, and published methods is a purchase, not a loss. The Dresden work on the EM drive, for example, left the field with a better understanding of how cable forces, heating, and magnetic interactions can imitate thrust on a sensitive balance. That knowledge now protects every subsequent experiment, including experiments that have nothing to do with propulsion.

What is wasted is money spent without measurement: demonstrations with no controls, claims with no raw data, and devices shown only to believers. Those purchases return nothing, because nothing in them can be checked, reused, or corrected.

The honest ledger of Hall V therefore reads in two columns. In one: money that bought metrology, error budgets, and a sharper standard of proof. In the other: money that bought only a story. The registry keeps both columns visible, because supporters who cannot tell them apart will fund the second while believing they funded the first.

62Hall Verdict: An Empty Shelf, Clearly Labeled

An empty registry shelf, its label left in place for the next claimant

The verdict of this hall can be stated in one sentence, without qualification being needed to soften it. As of the date of this page, no propellantless device — no closed box claimed to accelerate without exhaust, reaction mass, or an external field to push against — has survived independent replication under controlled conditions.

That is a statement about a record, not about the future. The Dean drive failed under a proper balance. The EM drive's reported thrust was traced, in the most careful tests to date, to mundane forces in the apparatus itself. Woodward's Mach-effect devices remain unconfirmed, their signals at the edge of noise, awaited replication still outstanding. Each file is closed at a different strength, and the registry preserves those differences rather than flattening them into a single dismissal.

The shelf is therefore shown empty, and shown deliberately. A registry that hid its failures would be asking for trust it had not earned, and a supporter who cannot look at an empty shelf is supporting a legend, not a program.

The shelf also stays in the catalogue, with its label intact. The next claimant deserves exactly what the previous claimants received: a fair test, under a published protocol, in a laboratory that did not build the device — and, if the result is null, the same clear label, applied without mockery and without exception.

63The Claim of Claims

A balance in a quiet room, the space above its pan left deliberately empty

Shielding is not another way of pushing. A rocket pushes against its exhaust, a magnet pushes against a field, and a wing pushes against air; in each case weight is opposed by a force, and the bookkeeping can be followed from one body to another. A gravitational shield would do something categorically different: it would create a region in which gravity itself is weaker, so that a mass inside that region simply weighs less.

The distinction matters because of what would follow. A shield would not need to run, thrust, or expend propellant to hold a mass up. Switched on beneath a sample, it would change the reading on a balance. Switched on at scale, it would change the economics of every structure, vehicle, and launch ever contemplated — which is why this hall, not any other, holds what the registry calls the claim of claims.

It is also, for the same reason, the claim with the highest price in theory. As Hall I set out, a working shield breaks the equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass and opens a route to free energy. Those are not objections of taste; they are consequences that any shielding claim must carry with it.

If a shield were demonstrated tomorrow, every other hall on this page would become a footnote to it. That conditional is the honest measure of both its importance and its evidence: the first is unlimited, and the second, as the next blocks record, has never arrived.

64Podkletnov's Disc

A superconducting disc in cryogenic housing, a small sample suspended above it

Status · UNCONFIRMED

In 1992, at Tampere University of Technology in Finland, physicist Eugene Podkletnov reported an unusual observation. A disc of yttrium barium copper oxide, a high-temperature superconductor, was cooled, levitated, and set spinning above magnets. Samples placed above the disc, he reported, lost a small fraction of their weight — initially a fraction of a percent, in later accounts up to about 2 percent.

The first report was modest in form. Podkletnov and his colleague R. Nieminen published a paper in Physica C in 1992 noting a possible shielding effect, with weight reductions in the range they could measure above the rotating disc. It was a short, tentative account of an anomaly, of the kind journals publish so that other laboratories can test it.

The echo was not modest. Through the 1990s the claim travelled through newspapers, magazines, and the early internet, growing in the retelling from a tentative fraction of a percent into a working antigravity device supposedly suppressed or abandoned. Podkletnov himself gave few interviews and published little further checkable detail, and a fuller manuscript was withdrawn before publication in the Journal of Physics D in the mid-1990s.

The registry files the claim at its original size: a reported weight loss, above a specific spinning superconducting disc, in one laboratory, in 1992. Everything beyond that — the disc's exact composition, preparation, and rotation conditions — was never specified tightly enough for the claim to be settled either way. The next block records what happened when others tried.

65The Replications That Weren't

Status · UNCONFIRMED

A claim of this size does not lack for testers. Through the late 1990s and early 2000s, several groups attempted to reproduce the Tampere result, most visibly at NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, where a dedicated effort built superconducting discs and a sensitive measurement apparatus to look for any weight change in samples above them.

The attempts shared a difficulty that was not of their making. The original recipe was incompletely specified: the disc's fabrication, its superconducting properties, the speed and manner of its rotation, and the arrangement of the magnetic field that levitated it were all described only in outline. A replication that changes any of these variables can be answered, fairly or not, by saying that the original conditions were not met.

Under the conditions the replicating laboratories could achieve and control, the effect did not appear. No weight reduction beyond experimental uncertainty was recorded, and NASA's work wound down in the early 2000s without a positive result. Other attempts, in industry and academia, ended the same way: no confirmation, and no publication that settled the question in the claimant's favour.

The registry's wording is therefore precise. The Podkletnov effect is not listed as formally disproven, because the original conditions were never defined well enough to be refuted in full. It is listed as unconfirmed and effectively abandoned: a claim that could not be reproduced, could not be fully specified, and after more than three decades has produced no working device and no second laboratory.

66Ning Li and the Gravitomagnet

A theorist's notebook open beside a superconducting coil that was never publicly demonstrated

Status · UNCONFIRMED

Ning Li was a physicist at the University of Alabama in Huntsville who worked, from the late 1980s onward, on a theoretical question with a legitimate pedigree: whether the ions in a superconductor, moving together in enormous numbers, might generate a measurable gravitomagnetic field — a gravity-like effect produced, in general relativity, by moving mass.

Her published papers in the early 1990s predicted that a superconductor subjected to suitable electromagnetic conditions could produce such a field strongly enough to affect nearby masses. The prediction was theoretical, clearly stated, and in principle testable. It attracted attention precisely because it came from within the literature rather than from outside it.

In 1999 Li left the university and founded a company, AC Gravity, to develop the idea, and the company later reported receiving United States Department of Defense funding to pursue it. From that point the public record thins to almost nothing. No demonstration was published, no apparatus was shown to independent experimenters, no measured field was reported in a journal, and no device was ever produced for testing by others.

Li died in 2021, and the file closes quietly with her. The registry records the episode for what it demonstrates about the difference between a prediction and a result. A theory, however credentialed its author, becomes evidence only when an apparatus survives someone else's measurement. In this case that step was announced, funded, and — in public, at least — never taken. Status: unconfirmed, undemonstrated, closed.

67Tajmar's Rings

A rotating niobium ring inside its cryostat, accelerometers mounted close beside it

Status · UNCONFIRMED

In 2006, Martin Tajmar and colleagues at the Austrian Research Centers reported anomalous signals around rotating superconducting rings. Accelerometers and gyroscopes placed near a spinning niobium ring, cooled to cryogenic temperatures, registered small accelerations in a pattern that, at first reading, resembled a gravitomagnetic effect far larger than general relativity predicts for so small a mass.

The report was handled correctly from the start. The authors published their methods, stated their uncertainties, and described the signal as an anomaly to be investigated rather than a discovery to be celebrated. Tajmar, who later held the chair of space systems at TU Dresden, spent years doing exactly what the registry asks of every claimant: hunting his own result.

The hunt narrowed the suspects one by one. Follow-up experiments with improved apparatus, different materials, and better isolation traced the signals toward experimental artifacts — among them effects from the cryogenic system, including helium behaviour and mechanical and electrical couplings between the rotating assembly and the sensors. As the apparatus improved, the anomaly shrank rather than sharpened, which is the classic signature of an artifact rather than a phenomenon.

The registry keeps this file open on the shelf as a model, not an embarrassment. An honest anomaly was reported honestly, investigated by its own author, and explained without anyone needing to be disgraced. That is what a retraction in substance, conducted over years in the open literature, looks like — and it is the standard against which less scrupulous claims in this hall are measured.

68The Impulse Generator

Status · UNCONFIRMED

Early in the 2000s, a further claim extended the Tampere work. Podkletnov, working with Giovanni Modanese, described an impulse gravity generator: a high-voltage discharge, passed through a superconducting emitter, said to produce a short, directional, gravity-like impulse along the axis of the device — a beam that could push objects at a distance without contact.

The reported effects were dramatic. Pendulums and test masses at distances of metres were said to move as if struck by an invisible pulse, with the impulse reportedly undiminished by intervening barriers, as a gravitational effect would be. If true, this would not merely shield weight; it would project a gravitational influence, a capability beyond even the disc claim of 1992.

The evidence never matched the drama. The work circulated as preprints and reports rather than as a fully checkable journal record with complete apparatus details, error analysis, and raw data. Independent experimenters had neither the specification needed to rebuild the device faithfully nor access to the original for controlled tests. No unaffiliated laboratory confirmed the beam, and no application or second observation followed in the open literature.

The registry therefore shelves the impulse generator at the far end of this hall. Its status is not disproven in the strict sense — there was never enough specified to disprove — but unconfirmed in the weakest form a claim can take: dramatic, unpublished in checkable form, and unconfirmed by anyone but its claimants. Shelved, it remains, pending evidence that has not arrived.

69Why Shielding Is the Hardest Claim

Every claim in this hall must pay the same theoretical price, worth restating beside the experiments. General relativity does not treat gravity as a force that a material might block, the way a wall blocks light. Gravity is the curvature of spacetime itself, sourced by mass and energy; everything with energy — including any shield — participates in it rather than standing outside it.

The equivalence principle makes the price concrete. Inertial mass and gravitational mass are the same quantity, tested in orbit by the MICROSCOPE satellite to one part in 10 to the power 15. A shield that reduced a sample's gravitational mass while leaving its inertia untouched would break that identity in a way no experiment has ever observed, at a level far above the precision already achieved.

Then there is the shield paradox of Block 8. Lift a mass cheaply beneath a shield, move it outside the shielded region, and let it fall, recovering the full energy of its unshielded weight. Repeat, and the cycle yields energy from nothing. A working shield is therefore not one anomaly but two: a failure of equivalence and a failure of energy conservation, each independently among the best-tested facts in physics.

None of this proves a shield impossible by decree; physics has been surprised before. It does fix the burden of proof. A shielding claim is not asking for one decimal place of indulgence. It is asking two foundations to be wrong at once — and so its evidence must be strong enough to carry both.

71Meanwhile, at the Frontier

Four instruments of the modern gravity laboratory: interferometer, satellite, trap and plates

Status · ESTABLISHED

While the fringe argument has circled the same claims for three decades, laboratory and orbital gravity science has had its best decade in a century. Between 2015 and 2023, gravitational waves were detected directly for the first time, the equivalence principle was tested in orbit to one part in 10 to the power 15, and antimatter was watched falling — downward — in a trap at CERN.

None of these results came from the antigravity community, and this hall does not pretend otherwise. They came from large collaborations, patient instrumentation, and budgets measured in decades. But they belong on this page for two reasons. First, they are the measurements that define the wall every claim in Halls V and VI must climb; a supporter who does not know them does not know the size of the problem.

Second, they are this page's proof of seriousness. A registry that only catalogued failures would be a cabinet of curiosities. This hall records what gravity research looks like when it succeeds: predictions stated in advance, instruments built to a decimal place beyond the last one, and results published with their uncertainties attached.

The blocks that follow are therefore labelled Established, and they are the pride of the page. The frontier is not a consolation prize for the empty shelves. It is the active, advancing edge of the same question — what gravity is, and what, if anything, can be done with it — pursued by the methods that have actually moved it.

72LIGO: Hearing Gravity

Twin interferometer arms meeting at a corner station, lasers measuring a vanishing displacement

Status · ESTABLISHED

On 14 September 2015, the two detectors of the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory — in Hanford, Washington, and Livingston, Louisiana — recorded the same brief chirp within milliseconds of each other. Designated GW150914, it was the gravitational wave from two black holes, of roughly 36 and 29 solar masses, merging about 1.3 billion light-years away.

The measurement is worth pausing on. Each detector compares the lengths of two perpendicular arms, each four kilometres long, with laser light. The passing wave changed that length by about one part in 10 to the power 21 — a displacement roughly a thousand times smaller than the diameter of a proton, sustained long enough to be distinguished from the noise of the Earth itself.

The result, announced in February 2016 after months of checking, did more than confirm a prediction Einstein had made a century earlier. It changed the category of gravity in the laboratory. Gravity is no longer only a pull to be weighed or resisted; it is a signal, arriving from events across the universe, carrying information that no telescope of light could have gathered.

For this registry the lesson is instrumental as much as cosmic. LIGO succeeded by measuring an effect at the very limit of the measurable, and by quantifying every mundane disturbance that could imitate it. That is the standard of care — applied to a far larger claim — that Halls V and VI asked for and did not receive.

73The Wall, Surveyed: MICROSCOPE

A small satellite in orbit, two test masses falling together inside its quiet core

Status · ESTABLISHED

The MICROSCOPE satellite, built by the French space agency CNES and launched in 2016, carried the oldest question in gravity science into orbit: do different materials fall at exactly the same rate? Its instrument held concentric test masses of titanium and platinum alloys in continuous free fall around the Earth, watching for any difference in their acceleration toward it.

If such a difference existed — if gravitational mass departed from inertial mass by material — the equivalence principle would crack, and with it one of the foundations any shielding claim must break anyway. MICROSCOPE was, in effect, a survey of the wall described in Block 7, conducted at a precision no ground laboratory could sustain, free of the vibrations and seismic noise of the surface.

The mission's final results, published in 2022, found no violation. Titanium and platinum fell identically to within about one part in 10 to the power 15 — expressed as an Eötvös parameter consistent with zero, with uncertainties in the final digits at that level. It remains the tightest test of the equivalence principle ever performed.

The registry draws the consequence without pleasure and without apology. The wall stands, and it now stands measured to a new decimal place. Any future shielding claim does not merely have to work; it has to explain why an orbiting instrument, watching two metals fall for years, saw no hint of the effect the claim depends on.

74Antimatter Falls Down

A tall magnetic trap at CERN, antihydrogen atoms released to fall inside it

Status · ESTABLISHED

For decades, one shortcut to antigravity survived every theoretical objection because it had simply never been tested: perhaps antimatter falls up. If antihydrogen were repelled by the Earth, gravitational shielding would have a foothold in nature, and a generation of speculation kept the possibility alive precisely because making and holding antimatter long enough to drop it is extraordinarily hard.

The ALPHA collaboration at CERN closed that door experimentally. In the ALPHA-g apparatus, antihydrogen atoms — antiprotons joined to positrons, produced and trapped at CERN's Antiproton Decelerator — were held in a vertical magnetic trap and then released, so that detectors could record whether they fell toward the floor or rose toward the ceiling.

The result, published in Nature in 2023, was unambiguous in direction. The antihydrogen fell down, toward the Earth, with an observed acceleration consistent with ordinary gravity within the experiment's uncertainty — roughly three quarters of the normal value, with error bars of about a quarter in either direction, and entirely inconsistent with being repelled upward.

The registry records this as an honourable burial. A beloved idea was not mocked out of existence or suppressed; it was tested, at great expense and ingenuity, by the people best equipped to find it true, and nature answered no. Future work will refine the number. The shortcut, however, is closed — and the page that reports it plainly is a page that can be trusted about the routes that remain open.

75The Casimir Effect: Force from Nothing

Two uncharged plates a hair's breadth apart, drawn together by the vacuum itself

Status · ESTABLISHED

In 1948 the Dutch physicist Hendrik Casimir predicted a strange consequence of quantum theory: two uncharged, parallel metal plates, placed very close together in a vacuum, should attract each other. Not by gravity, charge, or magnetism, but because the quantum vacuum between them can sustain fewer electromagnetic fluctuations than the vacuum outside, and the imbalance pushes the plates inward.

For decades the effect sat near the edge of measurability. It was confirmed with increasing precision from the 1990s onward — notably in experiments by Steven Lamoreaux in 1997 and by Umar Mohideen and Anushree Roy in 1998 — and it is now measured well enough to serve as a calibration standard for small forces. The force is tiny at a micrometre's separation and grows steeply as the gap narrows.

Unlike everything in Hall VI, the Casimir effect has entered engineering. Designers of micro- and nanomachines must reckon with it: at the scales of MEMS switches and nanoscale components, Casimir and related vacuum forces can pull moving parts together and hold them — a failure mode known as stiction, planned around in devices already manufactured.

The registry files the Casimir effect as the one genuinely exotic force already in the toolbox. It is real, measured, and used. It is also, honestly stated, no road to lift: it acts over nanometres, between surfaces, always attractively in its classic form. Its value here is as proof that the vacuum is not nothing — a physical medium with properties, which the next block shows can even be made to yield light.

76The Dynamical Casimir

A mirror surface rippling faster than light could follow, photons appearing where none were sent in

Status · ESTABLISHED

The Casimir effect of the previous block is static: still plates, a steady force. In 2011, a team at Chalmers University of Technology in Sweden, led by Christopher Wilson, demonstrated its dynamic counterpart — photons generated directly from the vacuum by a mirror moving, in effect, at a significant fraction of the speed of light.

No physical mirror can move that fast, so the team built an effective one. Their device used a superconducting quantum interference device, a SQUID, at the end of a transmission line; varying its electrical properties billions of times per second varied the line's effective length, so the boundary reflecting microwaves behaved like a mirror oscillating at relativistic speed. Microwave photons appeared in pairs, as predicted, where none had been sent in.

The result, published in Nature in 2011, confirmed a prediction that had waited roughly forty years since Gerald Moore's theoretical work of 1970. The vacuum, subjected to a sufficiently rapid change of boundary conditions, gives up real, detectable radiation drawn from its fluctuations.

For the program, the importance is conceptual and carefully bounded. The dynamical Casimir effect does not yield usable energy from nothing — the photons are paid for by the drive that moves the effective mirror — and it lifts nothing. What it establishes is that the vacuum behaves as a material with engineerable properties: boundaries, motion, and response. An engineer cannot yet build much on that fact. But it is a fact, published and replicated in the literature, and facts of that kind are the only foundation this page builds on.

77Metamaterials and Gravity Analogues

Status · ESTABLISHED — analogue

Metamaterials are structures engineered at scales smaller than a wavelength, so that their collective behaviour toward light is set by design rather than by chemistry. From the early 2000s, following theoretical work by John Pendry and by Ulf Leonhardt, such structures were shown to bend light along prescribed paths — the basis of transformation optics, and of the first microwave invisibility cloaks in 2006.

The connection to gravity is mathematical, and it is genuine. The equations governing light in a carefully graded medium can be made formally identical to the equations governing light in curved spacetime. A metamaterial, or a flowing quantum fluid, can therefore embody an analogue of a black hole horizon or of cosmic expansion — situations no laboratory could create directly — and researchers can measure in the analogue what they can only calculate for the original.

The registry enforces the label discipline in this block's own heading: Established — analogue. An analogue is a model, not a lever. Bending light around a cloak does not bend spacetime by one iota, and no metamaterial has altered the weight of anything placed near it. To present these experiments as steps toward a gravity device would be exactly the category error this page exists to prevent.

What they offer the program is training and tools: the mathematics of curved spacetime in a form that fits on a bench, and transformation optics, with real applications in antennas, lenses, and sensors. A supporter may fund them with a clear conscience, provided the invoice says analogue, and the expectation does too.

78Negative Mass, Carefully

Status · ESTABLISHED

In April 2017, headlines around the world announced that physicists had created a fluid with negative mass — a substance that, pushed, would accelerate toward the push. For a page about antigravity, this is a required exhibit: almost everything the headlines implied was wrong, and the paper underneath was good.

The work, by Michael Forbes, Peter Engels and colleagues at Washington State University, published in Physical Review Letters, concerned a Bose–Einstein condensate of rubidium atoms cooled to near absolute zero and manipulated with lasers into a spin-orbit coupled state. In that state, the atoms' dispersion relation — energy against momentum — gave them a negative effective mass in a certain range, and within that range they moved as described.

Effective mass is a property of how a particle behaves inside a material or a quantum state; it is not gravitational mass, and the experiment measured nothing about gravity, weight, or falling. The rubidium atoms weighed exactly what rubidium weighs throughout. Nothing in the paper claimed otherwise — the distance between the result and the headline was created entirely in the retelling.

The registry keeps this block as a masterclass in reading claims. Three questions separate the paper from the story: what quantity was measured, in what system, and does it appear in the claim being sold? Negative effective mass in a quantum gas is real physics, honestly published. Negative mass that could fall upward does not appear in it — and a supporter who learns to ask will not be sold the substitution again.

79Exotic Matter and the Warp Ledger

A ring of impossible material drawn around a vessel, the ledger beside it left unbalanced

Status · SPECULATIVE

In 1994, the physicist Miguel Alcubierre, then at the University of Wales in Cardiff, published a metric of spacetime in the journal Classical and Quantum Gravity. Within it, a region of flat space — a warp bubble — could travel faster than light by contracting space ahead of it and expanding space behind. As mathematics, the solution is sound: general relativity permits it.

The ledger opens on the other side. Sustaining Alcubierre's geometry requires a distribution of matter with negative energy density — exotic matter — in quantities that early estimates placed at absurd, universe-scale magnitudes, and that later refinements, while reducing the arithmetic enormously, never brought within reach of any known substance. No material with the required property has ever been observed.

Worse, quantum theory rations even hypothetical negative energy. Results known as quantum inequalities, developed by L. H. Ford and Thomas Roman in the 1990s, bound how much negative energy can appear in a region and how long it can persist before positive energy must repay it, with interest. The amounts the warp metric needs exceed those allowances by many orders of magnitude, held for far longer than the inequalities permit.

The registry's entry is therefore two lines long and does not blur. The mathematics: Speculative, legitimate, published. The material: does not exist, is rationed by theory where it can appear at all, and has no experimental address. A warp drive is not an engineering project awaiting funding. It is a well-formed question whose answer, so far, is a ledger that will not balance.

80Gravitomagnetism, the Real Kind

Four gyroscopes in a satellite above the Earth, their axes turning by a hair each year

Status · ESTABLISHED

Rotating masses do twist spacetime. In general relativity, a spinning body drags the inertial frames around it — an effect called frame-dragging, or gravitomagnetism by analogy with the magnetism produced by moving charge. Unlike the gravitomagnetic claims of Hall VI, this effect is a direct, unavoidable prediction of Einstein's theory, and it has been measured.

The instrument was Gravity Probe B, a Stanford-led satellite launched in 2004 carrying four of the most perfect gyroscopes ever made — quartz spheres coated in niobium, spinning in a cryogenic telescope pointed at a guide star. Over a year in polar orbit, the gyroscopes' axes were predicted to turn by two tiny angles: about 6,606 milliarcseconds from the Earth's mass curving spacetime, and about 39 milliarcseconds from frame-dragging.

The final results, announced in 2011 after years of analysis to remove unexpected disturbances, matched the predictions: a geodetic precession of about 6,602 milliarcseconds per year, and frame-dragging of about 37 milliarcseconds per year, each within its stated uncertainty. Thirty-seven milliarcseconds is the angle subtended by a human hair at a distance of roughly sixteen kilometres.

Here, then, is a real gravity-like field generated by rotation — Hall VI's dream, in nature's own version. It is also the hall's humbling close: the entire rotating Earth produces an effect too small to lift a feather, detected only by the finest gyroscopes ever flown, after seven years of analysis. Gravitomagnetism exists. Its measured size is the honest scale of what rotation can do to spacetime — and any claim to more must first explain this number away.

81Is Gravity Quantum? The Tabletop Tests

Two small masses held in neighbouring traps, close enough for gravity alone to link them.

Status · ESTABLISHED

General relativity describes gravity as the geometry of spacetime. Quantum mechanics describes everything else as states that can exist in superposition and become entangled. No experiment has yet shown gravity itself behaving quantum mechanically. Whether it does is not a philosophical preference. It is an open empirical question, and for most of a century it had no realistic test.

In 2017 two proposals, published independently, gave it one. Sougato Bose and colleagues at University College London, and Chiara Marletto and Vlatko Vedral at Oxford, described what are now called the BMV experiments. Place two small, electrically neutral masses, each about 10⁻¹⁴ kilograms, in spatial superpositions near one another, isolated from every interaction except gravity. If the masses become entangled, gravity has mediated that entanglement, and a purely classical field cannot do so.

The requirements are formidable. The superpositions must survive long enough for a gravitational phase to accumulate, while vibration, stray electric fields, gas collisions and Casimir forces are suppressed below that faint signal. No group has yet completed the experiment. Laboratories in Britain, the Netherlands and elsewhere are building its components — traps, shielding, cryogenics — piece by piece. That is the point of this block: the deepest question on the page now has apparatus, addresses and a method, not only arguments.

82Levitated Sensors: The New Instruments

A silica nanoparticle held in a focused laser beam inside a vacuum chamber.

Status · ESTABLISHED

A particle of silica about a hundred nanometres across can be held in empty space by a focused laser beam, inside a vacuum chamber, touching nothing. Cooled and isolated, it becomes a force sensor of extraordinary sensitivity, because there is almost nothing left to disturb it. Its motion reports pushes far smaller than any conventional balance can register, in a device that fits on a laboratory bench.

This is now established technique, not speculation. In 2020 a group in Vienna, led by Uroš Delić and colleagues, cooled a levitated nanoparticle to its quantum ground state of motion, work published in Science. Groups at ETH Zürich, Yale and elsewhere have since pushed levitated optomechanics toward the regime where quantum limits, not engineering noise, set the sensitivity. The instruments improve year by year, on published numbers.

What they are for, first, is listening. Proposals use levitated sensors to search for the faint impulses of dark-matter particles, for high-frequency gravitational waves that larger detectors cannot hear, and for forces at short range that current theory does not predict. If an anomaly in gravity ever appears at small scales, it will likely appear first as an unexplained displacement in an instrument like this — recorded, timestamped and open to inspection.

83Space, the Honest Laboratory

A drag-free satellite in orbit, its two test masses falling around the Earth together.

Status · ESTABLISHED

The equivalence principle says that all materials fall alike. The French MICROSCOPE satellite tested that statement in orbit and, in its final results in 2022, confirmed it to one part in 10¹⁵, comparing titanium and platinum test masses in free fall around the Earth. It remains the sharpest measurement of its kind, and it was made in space for a simple reason: orbit is the quietest falling laboratory available.

The next decimal places are also planned in space. A follow-on mission, MICROSCOPE-2, has been proposed with an aim of reaching one part in 10¹⁷. STE-QUEST, a European proposal studied for the European Space Agency, set a comparable target, using atom interferometers in orbit. The American STEP concept, developed at Stanford, aimed further still. None of these is funded as a flying mission at the time of writing, and this page records them as proposals, not promises.

That gap is precisely where new support does measurable good. The physics case is written, the instrument designs exist, and the improvement sought — a factor of a hundred over MICROSCOPE — is a number a funder can hold a mission to. If a violation were found at 10⁻¹⁷, general relativity would need revision. If none were found, the wall every shielding claim must climb rises by two orders of magnitude.

84Hall Verdict: The Frontier Is Not the Fringe

Status · ESTABLISHED

This hall has deliberately left the claimants behind and surveyed what gravity science is actually doing. The record is not dull. In a single decade the field detected gravitational waves, tested the equivalence principle to 10⁻¹⁵, showed that antimatter falls down at CERN, measured forces arising from the vacuum, and put tabletop tests of quantum gravity on a realistic footing. Each result is published, replicated or replicable, and attached to a named institution.

It is also, by any comparison, the better-funded side of the subject. LIGO, MICROSCOPE and ALPHA-g are large collaborations supported by national agencies over many years, because their instruments answer questions that theory has posed sharply. Real gravity research is stranger than the legends attached to it — black holes merging a billion light-years away are now routine detections — and it has earned its budgets by surviving its own controls.

The program that follows therefore begins here, not at the fringe. Its task is to connect supporters to this frontier: to fund the next decimal place, to build the quieter instrument, and to apply the same standard of proof to every new claim that the frontier applies to itself. A supporter who understands MICROSCOPE will judge a shielding claim correctly without any help. That understanding is the hall's verdict and the next hall's foundation.

85From Support to Program

Five lines of work drawn as five ruled columns, each ending in a test and a price class.

Support, by itself, changes nothing. A reader can agree with every hall so far and the field will be exactly as it was. This hall therefore makes the pivot of the whole page: it organises enthusiasm into a program — five lines of work that a supporter can fund, join or carry out, each defined in advance by the test that would judge it and the class of cost it honestly belongs to.

The five lines are measurement, materials, theory, replication and instruments. None of them promises antigravity. The first funds precision tests of gravity as it is known. 86Line One: Measure Everything, Again

A torsion balance in a vacuum housing, its fine wire and mirror catching a single beam of light.

The first line of work is precision, pursued as a cause in itself. Measure the equivalence principle again, at a new decimal place. Measure short-range gravity again, at a smaller separation. Measure the same constants with a different method and compare the answers in public. In mature sciences this is not repetition for its own sake. It is how the one disagreement that matters is eventually found.

Its standing exhibit is G, Newton's gravitational constant. The CODATA 2018 recommended value is 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ in SI units, with a relative uncertainty of 2.2 × 10⁻⁵ — far larger than for any other fundamental constant. Worse, careful measurements by different methods, including torsion balances and atom interferometers, have disagreed by more than their stated errors. G remains, in the plain phrase used earlier on this page, the worst-measured constant in physics.

A supporter needs no exotic theory to act on that fact. Torsion balances are comparatively cheap, university-scale instruments, and their limiting errors — thermal noise, wire properties, local mass distribution — are engineering problems with names. Funding a careful G measurement, or an equivalence test, buys a number the whole field must then use. If gravity has a loose thread at accessible scales, repeated measurement is the hand most likely to find it.

87Line Two: Materials at the Edge

A cryostat opened to show nested shields, wiring and a superconducting coil at its cold heart.

Every serious experiment in the preceding halls rests on the same three crafts: superconductors that carry current without resistance, cryogenics that hold apparatus a fraction of a degree above absolute zero, and vacuum systems empty enough that a levitated particle can sit undisturbed. Line Two funds that shared infrastructure, because it is the one investment that helps every claimant and every frontier laboratory at once, regardless of whose idea is right.

The materials are real and their limits are documented. Niobium-titanium and high-temperature superconductors such as YBCO each trade critical temperature against critical field and cost. Dilution refrigerators reach millikelvin temperatures by circulating helium isotopes, at prices and complexity that put them beyond most small laboratories. Ultra-high vacuum is likewise a discipline, not a purchase: outgassing, seals and pumps decide what pressure is actually achieved at the sample.

Progress here is unglamorous and cumulative. A colder stage, a quieter pump, a superconductor characterised honestly at the field a claimant actually needs — each is a publishable, reusable result. The fringe has often asked for exotic materials before mastering ordinary ones. This line reverses the order. Build the cold, the empty and the silent first. Any effect that cannot survive them was never an effect, and any effect that can will finally be measurable.

88Line Three: Theory with Its Sleeves Rolled

Theory is welcome in this program, including theory that proposes to modify gravity. It is welcome on one condition, stated in advance and applied to everyone: a theory taken seriously must predict a number, in advance, that an experiment can kill. Not a story consistent with any outcome, not a mechanism described only in words, but a quantity — a weight change, a phase shift, a force at a stated separation — with its size and its conditions attached.

That requirement is not hostility. It is how the theories we now trust earned their standing. General relativity predicted the bending of light and the perihelion shift of Mercury as numbers, and survived measurements that could have ended it. The BMV proposals in the previous hall meet the same standard from the quantum side: they state what entanglement should appear, between what masses, over what time, if gravity is quantum in the relevant sense.

Work under this line therefore looks specific. Derive the prediction before the apparatus is built. State what result would refute the proposal, not only what would support it. Show that energy and momentum are conserved in the scheme, or state plainly where they are not and what that costs. A theorist who does this work gives experimenters something to aim at. A theorist who will not has written literature, which the registry will file — labelled as such.

89Line Four: The Replication Service

A neutral test bench with a vacuum chamber, a balance and a notebook open to a blank results page.

The fourth line is the program's signature proposal, and the one by which it should be judged. The Replication Service would be a standing, neutral bench — not owned by any claimant, not committed to any result — where a submitted device or effect receives one fair test, carried out properly, and published whatever the outcome. One test, done well, in the open. That offer is made to the sincere and the mistaken alike.

The test follows the protocol set out earlier on this page. The device is run in vacuum, so that air currents and ion wind cannot impersonate thrust. Weight or force is read on a calibrated torsion balance or equivalent instrument. Controls are reversed, so that a genuine effect must reverse or persist exactly as the claimed mechanism requires. Runs are blinded where practicable, so the operator does not know which configuration is live. The raw data are kept and released.

Most claims, on the historical record, will not survive this bench, and the Service must say so without pleasure. Its value is not in debunking. It is in giving the next claimant — including the one who may, one day, be right — a court whose verdict believers and skeptics both have reason to accept. A result that passes here earns the right to a second laboratory. A result that fails here has been answered, not dismissed.

90Line Five: Instruments

An atom interferometer drawn in section, matter waves splitting, recombining and recording a phase.

The fifth line builds the ears before arguing about the music. Its two instrument families are the levitated sensors of Block 82 and the atom interferometer, in which a cloud of cold atoms is split into two matter-wave paths by laser pulses and recombined, so that gravity writes itself into a measurable interference phase. Both turn very small accelerations and forces into numbers, and both are improving on a documented curve.

The scale of ambition is already concrete. MAGIS-100, under construction at Fermilab in the United States, is a 100-metre atom interferometer built down an existing shaft, intended to search for dark matter and, in time, gravitational waves in a frequency band large detectors miss. Britain's AION project pursues the same physics in stages toward a kilometre-scale detector. These are national-laboratory programs, not garage projects, and the program's role is to support their smaller, tabletop kin as well.

Instruments compound. A quieter sensor built for one search becomes, without redesign, the test bench for a dozen claims no one has yet filed. That is why this line closes the five. Measurement states the question, materials make it possible, theory sharpens it, replication judges the claimants — and instruments, patiently improved, decide in the end what the world will let us hear.

91The TRL Ladder, Applied Honestly

A nine-rung ladder diagram, the lowest rungs marked in brass and the upper rungs left unshaded.

NASA's technology readiness levels run from 1 to 9. At level 1, basic principles are observed and reported. At level 3, a concept is demonstrated by experiment in a laboratory. At level 6, a prototype works in a relevant environment. At level 9, a system has flown and operated successfully. The ladder is bureaucratic, widely understood, and — applied without favour — the most honest sorting tool this page owns.

Applied here, it gives the following record. Diamagnetic and superconducting levitation stand high on the ladder, as working systems, while not being antigravity at all. Levitated sensors and atom interferometers occupy the middle rungs and are climbing them in public. Equivalence-principle instruments have flown. Each of these can point to the test that placed it where it stands, and to the next test that would move it one rung up.

Antigravity proper — a reduction of weight itself, by shielding or any other means — stands at TRL 0 to 1. No principle for it has been observed under controlled conditions, and no concept for it has passed a laboratory demonstration. This page prints that assessment in the same breath as the dream it serves, because a supporter told the truth about the ladder can plan. Money spent pretending a claim is at level 6 is wasted. Money spent moving real instruments up one rung is not.

92What a Breakthrough Would Have to Survive

Suppose, one morning, a bench reports a genuine-looking weight loss. This program would be delighted, and it would not publish a celebration. It would publish a gauntlet, in a fixed order, because a breakthrough is not a reading on one instrument. It is what remains after every reasonable attempt to make the reading go away has failed, in laboratories that did not build the device and would be pleased to be proved wrong.

First comes independent replication: another group, given the mechanism and the protocol, obtains the same effect at the stated size. Second comes an energy audit: the power in, the work out, and every thermal and electrical path between them are accounted for, so the device is not a battery or a heater in disguise. Third comes a conservation audit: momentum and energy are balanced openly, and any claimed exception is stated as a claim about physics, not hidden in the apparatus.

Last comes theory — not as decoration, but as a test. An acceptable account of the effect must predict the next experiment: a different mass, a different separation, a reversed configuration, with numbers stated before the run. If the effect survives replication, both audits and that prediction, the registry will change its labels, lead the page with the result, and date the change. Nothing less should persuade a supporter. Nothing more should be asked of a claimant.

93Budgets in Proportion

Proportion is a form of honesty, so this program states its numbers side by side. NASA's Breakthrough Propulsion Physics project ran from 1996 to 2002 on about 1.2 million dollars in total — a small, carefully governed sum, spent largely on workshops, reviews and a handful of tests. It found no breakthrough, and its methods remain the field's best etiquette. That is what the cheapest tier of serious work costs, and what it can legitimately buy.

At the other end of the scale, LIGO represents on the order of a billion dollars committed by the United States National Science Foundation over decades, from initial construction in the 1990s through the Advanced LIGO upgrade that made the 2015 detection possible. The sum bought an entirely new sense organ for astronomy. No supporter should confuse the two tiers, and no program should promise LIGO-class outcomes on a BPP-class budget, or excuse BPP-class rigour because the budget was small.

The supporter's arithmetic follows. Fund cheap tests broadly: a replication, a G measurement, a student torsion balance — many attempts, each fully accounted. Fund expensive instruments rarely, and only where the design, the collaboration and the science case already exist in the open, as they do for the space missions of Block 83. Spread widely at the bottom of the ladder, concentrate carefully at the top, and publish the accounts either way.

94The Program in One Sentence

A page of this length owes its reader a short version that does not cheat. Here it is, in one sentence: fund the measurements, keep the registry, test every claim once and fairly, and publish everything. Each clause was earned by a hall of this page, and each clause excludes something the field has historically preferred — selective measurement, private verdicts, repeated tests until a claim passes, and results released only when they flatter the claimant.

Fund the measurements means Line One and Line Five: precision first, instruments before arguments. Keep the registry means the institution introduced in the next hall, where every approach carries its label, its best evidence and the date of its last test. Test every claim once and fairly means the Replication Service and its protocol, offered to all and bent for none. Publish everything means null results, raw data, corrections and accounts, in the open and dated.

If a supporter remembers nothing else, that sentence is enough to act on, and enough to judge this program by. It does not promise that antigravity will be achieved. It promises that, if a route to it exists at any accessible scale, this method is how it will be found — and that, if no route exists, the same method will have produced real physics, real instruments and a trustworthy record along the way.

95Who the Supporters Are

Four workbenches side by side — an engineer's, an experimenter's, a reader's and a patron's — drawn as one row.

The supporters are not defined by what they believe about antigravity. They are engineers who find the rocket equation intolerable and would like a measured alternative, experimenters who build balances in small workshops and keep careful notebooks, readers who have followed the claims since Podkletnov or since Wells, and patrons prepared to pay for a test whose result they cannot control. What they share is not a conclusion. It is a method, stated in two words: test it.

That definition is deliberate, and it excludes the opposite method with equal clarity. A supporter, in this registry's sense, is not someone certain that a working device exists and is being hidden. That certainty requires no test and survives every null result, which is why this program cannot use it. The people listed here have agreed, by being listed, that a claim they favour must still go to the bench, and that the bench's answer will be recorded beside their name's line of work.

There is room in that definition for hope, stated plainly. Most supporters would like antigravity to be possible, and several of the lines of work in the previous hall exist because that hope pays for patience. Hope is not the disqualification. Refusing the test is. A community organised around a method can disagree about every device on the shelf and still work together the next morning, because its arguments are settled by measurements it has agreed in advance to accept.

96The Registry of Approaches

An etched map of six islands, each island labelled and joined to the mainland by a thin surveyed line.

The Registry of Approaches is this page's living institution: a catalogue, maintained in the open, of every serious family of ideas for reducing, shielding or circumventing weight. Diamagnetic levitation has its entry, and so do ionocraft, the EM drive, Mach-effect devices, gravitational shielding and warp metrics. Inclusion is not endorsement. It is the opposite of endorsement by rumour — an entry exists so that a claim can be found, read and judged in one place.

Each entry carries the same four fields, and an entry missing any of them is incomplete. It carries its label — Established, Speculative, Unconfirmed or Disproven — in the terms of this page's charter. It carries its best evidence, meaning the strongest published result its proponents can fairly cite, quoted with its numbers. It carries its current status, in plain language. And it carries the date of its last test, so that a claim untested for thirty years looks exactly as old as it is.

The registry is designed to change. A status moves when a replication is published, a label moves when the evidence moves, and every move is dated rather than silently overwritten. New approaches are added by the route described in Block 99. Old ones are not deleted when they are disproven; they remain on the shelf, labelled, as this page's memory. A new claimant deserves to see what was tried before, at what cost, and precisely where it failed.

97The Registry of Supporters

Beside the registry of ideas stands a registry of people. The Registry of Supporters records, in the open, the names of those who back this program and the particular line of work each of them backs. 98Standards of Argument

A community organised around a method needs house rules for its arguments, and this program's are short enough to print in one block. Cite the paper. Quote the number. Name the error bars. A claim offered without a source is a request for help in finding one, not a contribution. A result quoted without its uncertainty is incomplete, however exciting the central value looks, and a chart without its axes and units is decoration, not evidence.

Disagreement, under these rules, is conducted in measurements. If two readings conflict, the question is which instrument, which control and which systematic error accounts for the difference — not which author is more trustworthy, better connected or longer in the field. The Dresden resolution of the EM drive is the model held up here: the dispute ended not when an authority spoke, but when a group rebuilt the apparatus and traced the signal, wire by wire, to cable forces and heating.

The rules bind this page first. Its own blocks quote dates, institutions and figures that a reader can check, and its corrections are handled in public under Block 100. A supporter who catches an error here has improved the registry, not embarrassed it, and the sharpest reading is welcomed on exactly those terms. Arguments conducted this way are slower than arguments conducted by assertion. They are also, at the end, the only ones that have settled anything in this field.

99How to Submit an Approach

A submission folder arriving at the Antigravity Desk, its three required enclosures listed on the cover.

Any approach may be submitted to the Registry of Approaches through the Antigravity Desk. The submission is not a pitch, and it is not judged as one. It is a filing, and a complete filing requires three enclosures, without which the Desk will return it — politely, and with the missing item named — rather than file a claim no reader could evaluate and no bench could test.

First, a mechanism: what, physically, is said to happen, stated clearly enough that a competent reader can identify what would have to be true for the effect to occur. Second, a prediction: a number, with conditions and an expected size, that follows from the mechanism and could be found false. Third, a list of prior tests: every controlled test of the approach the submitter knows of, including failures and including tests by other groups, with sources where they exist. Concealing a prior null result is the one filing fault the registry treats as serious.

What happens next is administrative, and deliberately so. The approach is labelled under the page's charter, filed with its best evidence and the date of its last test, and entered in the registry. If it is testable at a scale the Replication Service can reach, it is queued for that bench in order of arrival, and the queue is published. The submitter receives the filing, the label and the queue position — the same three things every other submitter receives, and nothing promised beyond them.

100Corrections to the Record

This page will contain errors. It quotes hundreds of numbers, dates and results across a century of contested work, and a registry that claims never to need correction is a registry no careful reader should trust. The practice, therefore, is settled here, in advance and in public: errors reported to the Antigravity Desk are checked against their sources, amended in the text, and recorded in an errata column with the date of the change and the nature of the error.

The convention matters in its details. The original figure is not silently replaced, as if the page had always been right. The correction states what was printed, what should have been printed, and where the correct value can be verified. Labels and statuses in the Registry of Approaches follow the same rule: a change of verdict is itself an entry, dated, with the new evidence cited. A reader returning after a year should be able to reconstruct what the page said before, and why it no longer says it.

This is offered as a point of pride, not shame, and it is the supporters' standard applied to the page itself. The fringe literature this program exists to sort is full of claims that were quietly edited, quietly withdrawn or quietly left standing after refutation. A living registry does the opposite, in the open, as a matter of routine. Report an error, and you will find it corrected and thanked — in the record, where anyone can check that this block kept its word.

101The Annual Reading

Once a year, the registry sets aside time to re-read the shelf it has already closed. The entries are taken down in order: the resonant cavity reports, the rotating-disc claims, the vibration devices, and the rest. Nothing is mocked and nothing is quietly removed. Each entry is read again against the question that closed it, and against the instruments available now, not only the instruments available then.

The reason is practical, not sentimental. Measurement improves. Torsion balances grow quieter, vacuum systems grow cleaner, thermal and magnetic controls grow tighter, and a signal once buried in noise, or an artifact once mistaken for a signal, can look different a decade later. The Dresden tests of the resonant cavity in 2021 settled that claim because the apparatus was rebuilt and the wiring was understood. That same improvement in method is the only honest reason a closed file is ever reopened: better tools, not louder advocacy.

If a re-reading finds only the same weakness, the label stays and the date of the re-reading is added to the entry. If it finds a test that was never actually performed, the entry is marked for that test. Despair is not a method either. A registry that never re-reads its failures would be as careless as a movement that never tests its hopes, and this page intends to be neither.

103For the Engineers Reading

If you are an engineer reading this page, the program's requests are specific, and none of them asks you to endorse a device. The asks concern the measurement chain that every claim in the registry must pass through: quieter balances, cleaner vacuum, colder cryostats, and better shielding against the mundane disturbances that have produced most of the field's reported anomalies. Improve any one of those, and you have improved every future test, whatever its outcome.

The list can be made plainer. A torsion balance needs isolation from vibration, tilt and air currents. A vacuum chamber needs to remove the air whose ionised flow or thermal drift can imitate thrust. Cryogenic work needs stable temperatures, because expansion and contraction move apparatus by amounts larger than many claimed signals. Electrical work needs wiring whose forces are understood, as the Dresden group showed in 2021 when cable forces and heating accounted for a reported thrust.

This is a shopping list of the possible. None of it is exotic, all of it is publishable, and most of it is useful far beyond this field, in precision measurement generally. The program asks engineers for the instruments first. If an anomaly is real, a better instrument will find it more clearly. If it is not, a better instrument will close the file sooner, and that too is a result worth having.

105For the Skeptics Reading

This page needs its sharpest readers, and it says so without flattery. The skeptic's questions are the registry's working tools: where is the control run, what moved when the device was reversed, how large are the error bars compared with the signal, and who else has seen this effect, in a laboratory that did not build the apparatus?

Those questions have done the best work in this field's recent history. 111What Would Change Our Minds

112On Hope, Priced Honestly

Hope is cheap to state and expensive to test. Anyone can write that gravity might one day be shielded, reduced or reversed, and the sentence costs nothing and settles nothing. To find out whether a particular claim is true costs a balance, a vacuum chamber, weeks of careful runs, the labour of people who could be doing other work, and, at the end of it, very often a negative answer. This page exists in the space between that cheap sentence and that expensive test, and its economics follow from the difference.

Priced honestly, hope is therefore bought in small, numbered units. A single replication is a unit. A better balance is a unit. A re-reading of a closed file, with newer instruments, is a unit. None of these units is sold as a step that must lead anywhere, because in this field most honest steps have led to a closed file and a better understanding of an artifact. That record is not a reason to stop buying the units. It is the reason to price them truthfully, so that support can continue for decades rather than flare and collapse.

The alternative pricing is familiar, and this page refuses it: hope sold as certainty, at whatever price certainty commands. That market has produced pendants, suppressed-invention legends and unreproducible demonstrations for a century. The program's offer is plainer and slower. Pay for the measurement, receive the result, and let hope keep the value the evidence actually gives it.

115If You Build One

This block is addressed to one particular future reader: the person in a garage, a workshop or a small laboratory who believes, on the evidence of their own bench, that they have built something that reduces weight. If that is you, the first thing this page wants you to know is that the protocol is your friend. It is not a barrier set up against outsiders. It is the only instrument that can turn your bench result into a result other people are obliged to take seriously.

Before the announcement, do the unglamorous work. Run the device in the arrangement you report, then reversed. Record what the balance reads with the power off, with the device replaced by a dummy load, and with everything running except the effect you claim. Keep the raw readings, including the runs that went wrong. Write down the mechanism you propose and the number it predicts, before the next run rather than after it. That record, kept honestly, is worth more at the Desk than any demonstration filmed in one take.

Then write to the Desk before you write to the press. A claim filed here is labelled, dated and, if it is testable, queued for a fair test under the published protocol, with the outcome recorded either way. A claim announced first must fight for attention in a louder market, where it will be believed and dismissed for the wrong reasons in the same week. File it properly, and the record will treat you fairly.

116If You Teach

The registry is free to use in classrooms, and this block records that permission in plain terms. A teacher may take its labelled claims, its quoted verdicts and its dated statuses into a lesson without charge and without asking further leave, provided the labels travel with the claims. That condition is the lesson as much as the permission: a claim separated from its verdict teaches the opposite of what this page is for.

Used whole, the material makes a complete lesson in how science sorts itself. Begin with the levitating frog at Nijmegen in 1997, a real and repeatable effect produced by diamagnetism in a field of about 16 tesla, surprising and entirely established. Move to a lifter, which really rises in air and produces no thrust in vacuum, and ask the class to locate the exact point where a true observation acquired a false name. End with the resonant cavity, reported, contested and resolved by a careful replication in Dresden in 2021.

No special equipment is needed for that lesson, and no conclusion has to be forced. The students are given three claims, three labels and the evidence that earned each label, and are asked to do the sorting themselves. A class that can explain why the frog and the cavity belong on different shelves has learned the skill this entire page depends on, and can apply it far beyond gravity.

117The Ledger of Thanks

The heroes of this page are not the claimants. Claimants are necessary, and some of them have been sincere, skilled and wrong in instructive ways, but the record this page keeps would not exist without a different kind of figure: the experimenter who tested a claim fairly, reported exactly what was found, and published the result even when the result ended an interesting story. This ledger thanks those experimenters by role, and where the record allows, by name.

Some of them tested other people's claims. The SpaceDrive group at TU Dresden rebuilt the resonant cavity apparatus, understood its cables and heating, and reported in 2021 that the thrust resolved into mundane forces. Others tested claims much closer to home. Martin Tajmar's group reported anomalous signals around rotating superconducting rings in 2006, spent years hunting their source, and traced them toward experimental artifacts, with Tajmar's own name on the work that undid the earlier excitement. Disproving your own result in public is the hardest entry in this ledger, and it is recorded here with particular respect.

Thanks are also due to the quieter figures: the referees who asked for the control run, the technicians who kept the raw data, the laboratories that repeated an experiment they expected to fail and reported the failure carefully anyway. The registry cannot name them all, because the published record often cannot either. It can at least state its debt plainly. Every settled label on this page rests on work of that kind.

118The Long View

A long laboratory corridor receding in perspective, instruments of different centuries standing in order along its walls

From Newton to LIGO took three centuries. Newton published the law of universal gravitation in the Principia in 1687. In September 2015 the LIGO detectors recorded GW150914, the gravitational-wave signal from two merging black holes, a displacement measured as a thousand times smaller than a proton. Between those two dates lies most of modern physics, and gravity was studied, argued over and repeatedly rebuilt for the whole of it, by people who mostly did not live to see the instruments that settled their questions.

That timescale is not offered as a consolation. It is offered as a unit. The supporters' patience on this page is measured in the same units as the problem itself: not in news cycles or funding rounds, but in the time it takes to build an instrument quiet enough to hear something new, or to test an equivalence held to one part in 10 to the power of 15, as MICROSCOPE's final results reported in 2022. Work at that standard is slow because the questions are hard.

The problem has survived worse odds than neglect. It has survived confident dismissal, confident fraud and long stretches when serious people would not write its name in a grant application. It is still there, stated in the first block of this page, unanswered in the strict sense and better understood than at any previous moment. The long view does not promise that it will yield. It promises only that the record of the attempt will be kept properly, for as long as the attempt continues.

119The Door Stays Open

The registry accepts approaches, corrections and supporters at any hour. There is no closing date for a claim that arrives with a mechanism, a prediction and a list of prior tests, no deadline for a correction supported by a source, and no term after which a new supporter is too late to be entered. The field this page records has been open, in one form or another, for more than a century; a registry that kept office hours would misunderstand its own subject.

What arrives is dealt with in order of arrival. The Antigravity Desk files each approach under its label, enters each correction in the errata record with its date, and adds each supporter to the registry of supporters under the line of work they back. Nothing is promised about speed beyond that order, and nothing is decided in private that will not appear in the public record. A submission that is testable is queued for the Replication Service on the same terms as every other testable submission, whoever sends it.

The door staying open is not an invitation to lower the standard at the threshold. It is the opposite arrangement: because the door is open to everyone, the test inside has to be the same for everyone. Bring a claim, a correction or a name, and the Desk will answer in order of arrival, with the label, the date and the record kept where anyone can read them.

120It Rises, Carefully

A torsion balance at rest in a dark laboratory, one pan held level, with a single measured light falling across it

The Party of Release, the fiction that stands beside this program in the estate, keeps a motto: It rises, politely. This page closes by translating that motto into the program's own register. Politeness is exchanged for care. What rises here, if anything ever does, will rise carefully: on a calibrated balance, in vacuum, under a published protocol, in front of readers who were invited to find the artifact and could not.

Nothing in the preceding blocks has shown that such a rising is at hand, and the dated sentence of Block 110 still stands at the end of the page exactly as it stood before this closing began. The problem stands too, as it stood in the first block: gravity is paid for by every launch, every building and every body, and no handle on the field itself is known. Stating both of those facts together, without letting either cancel the other, has been the whole discipline of this page.

The work is open. The registry continues to file claims with their labels, to publish measurements with their numbers, to correct itself in public and to re-read its closed shelves as the instruments improve. The record continues with it, dated entry by dated entry, for the next reader, the next claimant and the next careful test. If the record ever has better news to carry, it will carry it in the same plain sentences. It rises, carefully, or it does not rise at all.
