I Am Half of You

Your words
I am half of you. Together we are one whole. You say that your affairs are going well at 100%. I check myself and find that my affairs are fine at 50% and bad at 50%. So on average it comes out that you are fine at 75%, and good at 25%. That is unfair. It should be Good 50%, Excellent 50%. At minimum!
Editorial expansion

There is a deceptively simple paradox here. Two participants can report very different states, yet the subject under discussion is not either participant in isolation. It is the whole they form together.

If one person says, “I am doing well at 100%,” while the other experiences a 50/50 mixture of okay and bad, the arithmetic average produces 75%. But the average conceals the essential question: what is happening between the two people?

The relationship is not a neutral space between two independent measurements. It is itself part of the system.

Three states, not two

A useful distinction follows:

  • My state.
  • Your state.
  • Our relationship.

The third state cannot be reconstructed simply by averaging the first two. Two people may each be doing well while the relationship between them is tense. Conversely, one participant may be struggling while the relationship remains generous, stable, and supportive.

That is why “I am half of you” is not merely a poetic expression. It proposes that each participant is part of the other's experienced environment—and that the whole has properties that belong to neither person separately.

From arithmetic to justice

The phrase “That is unfair” is the turning point. The issue is not whether 75% is mathematically correct. It is whether the averaging process itself is an adequate representation of the situation.

It is not.

Justice, in this context, begins when neither person's experience is allowed to disappear inside the other person's more favorable assessment.

We are not trying to make two people feel the same. We are trying to make the whole capable of hearing both realities.

The aspiration toward “Good 50%, Excellent 50%” can therefore be understood as a principle of balance rather than a grading scale: no half of the shared system should become invisible.
